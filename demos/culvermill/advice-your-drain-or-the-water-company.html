Is it your drain or the water company's?

The question we get asked first, and the one that decides who pays. Here is the general rule and where it stops being simple.

Generated for this demo. Not a photograph of a real job.

This is the first question most people ask and it is the right one, because the answer decides who pays. The general position in England is simpler than it used to be, and it still has enough corners in it to be worth ten minutes of reading.

The general rule

Pipes inside your boundary that serve only your property are yours to look after. Once a pipe leaves your boundary, or once it serves more than one property, it is normally the water company's responsibility. That change happened in 2011, when a great many shared drains and lateral drains transferred across, and it took a lot of back runs on terraced streets off the householder and onto the company.

Your drainThe pipework inside your boundary serving only your house. The run from the toilet to the chamber by the back door, the gully outside the kitchen, the soakaway in your garden.
Shared, or past the boundaryA run serving more than one property, or any pipe beyond your boundary. Normally the water company's, including most back runs on a terrace.

Where it stops being simple

Four things muddy it. First, nobody knows where their boundary is underground as precisely as they think they do, and a chamber that looks like it is in your garden can be the other side of the line. Second, private sewage systems, so septic tanks and cesspits, were not part of that transfer and remain the owner's throughout. Third, a soakaway or a rainwater system that was built for your house alone stays yours wherever it runs. Fourth, newer estates sometimes have arrangements of their own, with a management company in the middle of it.

And then there is the ordinary confusion of an old street, where the drain plan either does not exist or shows something that was changed in 1974.

Why it matters before anyone starts work

If a blockage is on a stretch the water company looks after, they will generally come and clear it and there is usually nothing for you to pay. Ringing a drainage firm first and paying for that same clearance is a real thing that happens every week, and it is avoidable.

So the order to do things in is: look, find out which chamber is full, and work out roughly where that sits relative to your boundary. If it is at or past the boundary, or if your neighbours have the same problem at the same time, ring your water company before you ring anyone else. They have a number for exactly this and they answer it at all hours.

If we lift the covers and think the blockage is on their side of the line, we will say so and tell you to ring them. That is a short conversation and a job we do not get, and it is still the right answer.

What to find out, in order

  1. Which chamber is full. Lift them from the house outwards until you find the first one with standing water in it. The blockage is downstream of that.
  2. Whether anything is shared. Does your run take the neighbours' waste as well? On a terrace it very often does.
  3. Roughly where your boundary is on the ground. The deeds will say. The fence is not always it.
  4. Whether the neighbours have the same problem right now. If three houses on a run are all slow, it is the shared run, not your house.

Who to ring

Your local water company, for anything on a shared run or past the boundary, and for anything you think might be theirs. They are also the people to tell if waste is coming out of a manhole in the street, which is their job and nobody else's.

A drainage firm, for anything inside your boundary that serves only you, for surveys, for repairs, and for the times you want somebody to look and tell you which of the two it is.

And if it is a septic tank or a cesspit, remember the whole of that system is the owner's. There is no transfer and no company to ring about it.

Stop using the water, then ring

Everything that goes down a sink, a bath or a toilet ends up in the same run. If that run is blocked, every flush and every washing machine cycle adds to what has nowhere to go. Stop using the water in the house, then ring and tell us what you can see.

While you wait

  1. Do not flush again to see if it has cleared. A second flush on a blocked run is how it ends up on the floor.
  2. Lift the chamber cover outside if you can do it safely, and look. Full to the top means the blockage is further down the run. Empty means it is between the house and that chamber.
  3. Keep children and pets away from anything that has come up, and keep your hands out of it.
  4. Move what you can off the floor and put towels at the bathroom door.
  5. Take a photo of the chamber. It is the quickest way for us to know what we are coming to.

If waste water is coming up inside the house, or if it is near sockets or anything electrical, treat it as unsafe. Switch that circuit off at the board if you can reach it from somewhere dry, and tell us when you ring.
