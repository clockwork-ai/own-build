Five things that block drains, and how to stop them

Nearly everything we pull out of a drain is one of five things. Four of them you can do something about.

Generated for this demo. Not a photograph of a real job.

Nearly everything that comes out of a blocked drain is one of five things. Two of them are what went down it, two of them are what is wrong with the pipe, and the last one is usually funny afterwards.

1. Wipes

The biggest single cause now, and it was not twenty years ago. Toilet paper is designed to break apart in water within seconds. A wipe is designed not to, because its whole purpose is to stay in one piece while it is used. Flushable on the packet means it will go round the bend of the pan. It does not mean it will have broken up by the time it reaches a joint forty feet away.

What happens then is that one wipe catches on something rough, and then everything that comes past catches on the wipe. A month later there is a plug the size of a fist.

What stops it: a bin in the bathroom. That is the whole answer, and it is not a popular one, but there is no product that makes a wipe behave like paper.

2. Fat and grease

The other big one, and the one people genuinely do not realise they are doing. Cooking fat goes down the sink as a warm liquid with hot water behind it, and twenty feet along the run, in a cold pipe underground, it is a solid again. It sets on the pipe wall in a layer, the layer thickens, and the bore gets smaller every month until something catches in the narrow bit.

You can see it when a gully is lifted outside a kitchen window: a pale, waxy crust round the pot and over the outlet.

What stops it: a jug or an old tin by the hob, let it set, in the bin. Hot water and washing up liquid move the problem down the pipe rather than away.

3. Roots

Not your fault and not something you can prevent by being careful. A drain is the wettest, most nutritious thing within reach of a tree, and a root will find a hairline crack or an open joint and follow it in. It comes in as a fine beard and over a few years becomes a mass that fills the pipe.

You can tell roots from the way the blockage behaves: it comes back, it comes back at roughly the same time of year, and whatever is pulled out is fibrous rather than soft.

What stops it: closing the way in. Cutting roots out gets the drain running and they come back, because the crack is still there. The actual fix is a patch liner over the defect, and then they stop.

4. A belly in the run

The one nobody expects. Drains work on fall: a steady slope from the house to the sewer so that water runs and carries solids with it. If the ground under one length settles, which old made up ground and dug out gardens both do, that length loses its fall and water stands in it instead of running through.

Standing water drops whatever it is carrying. So every month a bit more settles in the dip, and the run gets more sensitive until almost anything blocks it.

What stops it: nothing you can do from the house. A survey finds it and measures it, and then it is a conversation about whether that length is worth relaying. Some bellies are small enough to live with and worth jetting now and then. Some are not.

5. Things that should never have been near a drain

Nappies, cat litter, cement washings down an outside gully, a builder's bucket of plaster, children's toys, and the single most common one, those cistern blocks that get knocked into the pan and flushed without anybody noticing. Each one is a one off and between them they are a decent fraction of the work.

What stops it: a grating that sits flat on the gully, a cover on the chamber, and knowing that if cement or plaster has gone down an outside drain, it wants flushing through right away rather than at the weekend.

How to tell which one you have

  • Blocked once, cleared, fine since. Something went down it. Nothing wrong with the pipe.
  • Blocks every few months. Something is wrong with the pipe. Fat on a rough joint, roots, or a belly.
  • Blocks at the same time each year. Usually roots, which are seasonal, or leaves in a gully.
  • Everything downstairs is slow at once. The shared run, not any one fitting.
  • One basin or bath only. A trap or a short branch, not the main run.

Which is the whole case for a camera. Clearing a drain tells you it was blocked. Looking at it tells you whether it is going to happen again, and that is the only question worth paying to answer.

Stop using the water, then ring

Everything that goes down a sink, a bath or a toilet ends up in the same run. If that run is blocked, every flush and every washing machine cycle adds to what has nowhere to go. Stop using the water in the house, then ring and tell us what you can see.

While you wait

  1. Do not flush again to see if it has cleared. A second flush on a blocked run is how it ends up on the floor.
  2. Lift the chamber cover outside if you can do it safely, and look. Full to the top means the blockage is further down the run. Empty means it is between the house and that chamber.
  3. Keep children and pets away from anything that has come up, and keep your hands out of it.
  4. Move what you can off the floor and put towels at the bathroom door.
  5. Take a photo of the chamber. It is the quickest way for us to know what we are coming to.

If waste water is coming up inside the house, or if it is near sockets or anything electrical, treat it as unsafe. Switch that circuit off at the board if you can reach it from somewhere dry, and tell us when you ring.
