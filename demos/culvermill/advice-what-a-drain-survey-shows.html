What a drain survey shows you

What a camera actually tells you, what the words on the report mean, and when a survey is money wasted.

Generated for this demo. Not a photograph of a real job.

A survey is a camera on a push rod, or a crawler for bigger pipe, fed along the drain while somebody watches the picture and notes the distance. That is all it is. What makes it useful is not the camera, it is the distances, because a defect with a distance on it is something a person with a spade can find again.

What it actually shows

  • Cracks and fractures. A line across or along the pipe. Some are hairline and stable, some are open and letting ground in, and the difference matters.
  • Open or displaced joints. Two lengths that have pulled apart. Soil washes in and a void forms outside the pipe, which is how paths and patios start to sink.
  • Roots. Coming in at a joint or a crack, fine at first and eventually filling the bore.
  • A belly. Standing water part way along a run where there should not be any. Measured by how deep the water sits.
  • Scale and fat. A build up on the pipe wall, which narrows the bore and gives everything else something to catch on.
  • The unexpected. A pipe cut through by a fence post, a run that goes somewhere nobody thought it did, and the occasional drain that was built over.

What the report will say

A decent report tells you four things about each thing it found: what it is, how far along the run it is, how serious it is, and whether it sits on your side of the boundary. If it tells you less than that, it is not much use to anybody.

  1. What and whereA cracked joint at nine metres, with a still of it. The distance is what turns a survey into a repair somebody can price.
  2. How seriousNeeds doing now, worth doing when convenient, or worth keeping an eye on. Not everything a camera sees is a problem.
  3. Whose it isInside your boundary serving only you, or shared and past it. That decides who the repair is a conversation with.
  4. What the options areA patch, a full liner, a jet now and then, or a dig. Usually more than one of those is reasonable.

When a survey is worth doing

When it keeps blocking. The clearest case. Paying to clear the same run three times in a year without once looking at why is the most expensive way to own a drain.

Before you buy a house. Particularly an older one with clay pipe and mature trees. It is a small cost against a collapsed run under a drive, and questions are far easier to raise before contracts than after.

When something near the run is sinking. A path, a patio, a garden wall or a bit of drive that has dropped. An open joint washing soil away underneath is a common cause and it will not stop on its own.

Before anybody digs. Including before you build over or near a run. A trench in the wrong place costs more than the survey, and building over a drain you have not looked at is a decision you will not be able to reverse cheaply.

When it is money wasted

When a drain blocked once, cleared, ran clean and has been fine since. Something went down it that should not have. The pipe is telling you it is fine by behaving itself. We will say so rather than sell you a survey, and we would be wary of anybody who would not.

Also when the run is still blocked. A camera in standing water shows you standing water. Clearing comes first, every time, which is why a survey after a blockage is usually the same visit and not a separate one.

One last thing. Ask to see the screen while the camera is still in the pipe. Everything is easier to ask about with the picture live in front of you than it is a week later looking at a file on a phone.

Written for this demo from general trade knowledge. It is not advice on your drain, and it is no substitute for somebody lifting a cover and looking.

Stop using the water, then ring

Everything that goes down a sink, a bath or a toilet ends up in the same run. If that run is blocked, every flush and every washing machine cycle adds to what has nowhere to go. Stop using the water in the house, then ring and tell us what you can see.

While you wait

  1. Do not flush again to see if it has cleared. A second flush on a blocked run is how it ends up on the floor.
  2. Lift the chamber cover outside if you can do it safely, and look. Full to the top means the blockage is further down the run. Empty means it is between the house and that chamber.
  3. Keep children and pets away from anything that has come up, and keep your hands out of it.
  4. Move what you can off the floor and put towels at the bathroom door.
  5. Take a photo of the chamber. It is the quickest way for us to know what we are coming to.

If waste water is coming up inside the house, or if it is near sockets or anything electrical, treat it as unsafe. Switch that circuit off at the board if you can reach it from somewhere dry, and tell us when you ring.
