Full replastering

Full replastering.

Old plaster off back to the brick, the background sorted out, a thick undercoat ruled dead flat, then a finish coat on top. The job for walls a skim would only follow the bumps of.

Why a wall comes off rather than gets skimmed

Three reasons, and the first is much the most common. The plaster has blown, which means it has lost its grip on the brick and sounds hollow when you tap it. That happens from damp, from a background that was dusty when it was first plastered, or just from age on a lime wall that has been there a hundred and twenty years. No finish coat sticks to something that is itself not stuck.

The second is wallpaper taking the plaster with it when it comes off, which tells you the same story. The third is a wall that is so far out of true that skimming it would just give you a smooth version of the same bow. A straight edge held against it settles that one in about four seconds.

What it actually involves

Hacking off is the messy, noisy part and there is no polite version of it. The old plaster comes off to the brick or block, the joints get raked out where the mortar is loose, and the whole wall gets brushed down. Then the background decides the undercoat: hardwall or browning on ordinary brickwork, bonding where the background is dense or patchy, lime where the house was built to breathe.

That undercoat goes on eight to eleven millimetres thick, in bands, and gets ruled off against the beads with a straight edge. All the flatness in the finished wall is made here. Then it gets keyed and left to set, properly, before the finish goes anywhere near it. Rushing that stage is the single most common cause of cracking later. Two to three days for a room, and the drying afterwards is two to three weeks rather than one.

Old houses and lime

If a house has solid walls with no cavity, it was built to let moisture travel through the wall and evaporate. Lime plaster lets that happen. Hard gypsum and cement do not, so the moisture goes somewhere else instead and comes out as damp lower down, or on the far side, or under the skirting. On a stone cottage or a pre war solid wall we will say that at the visit rather than quietly using whatever is on the van. It costs more and takes longer, and it is the right answer often enough that it is worth a conversation.

On the job

How this one usually goes.

  1. Protect everything, then hack offSheets, boards, doorway sheeted, and then the old plaster comes off to the background. Loud and dusty, and the only honest way to describe it.
  2. Sort the background outJoints raked where the mortar is soft, the wall brushed down, and any patches of different material dealt with so one undercoat can go over the lot.
  3. Beads, and the straight edgeAngle beads plumbed on the corners and the reveals. They are what the rule works to, so a crooked bead means a crooked corner forever.
  4. Undercoat on and ruled flatEight to eleven millimetres, ruled off against the beads, then keyed and left to set rather than hurried. This is where the flatness comes from.
  5. Finish coat, then clear upTwo passes of finish, troweled closed as it goes off. Floors scraped, waste taken away, and a straight answer on drying time before paint.

Questions

Asked about this job.

How do I know if my plaster has blown?

Tap it with your knuckles, moving across the wall. Sound plaster is dull and solid. Blown plaster sounds hollow, like a drum, and it often feels very slightly springy. Low down near the skirting and around old chimney breasts are the usual places to find it.

Is it very messy?

Hacking off is the messiest thing we do, and there is no point pretending otherwise. Sheets, boards and a sheeted doorway keep it to the room as much as anything can, and the waste leaves with us. Expect to live round one closed door for a couple of days.

How long before I can paint?

Two to three weeks for a fully replastered wall, sometimes longer on a thick undercoat in a cold month. It is not the finish coat that takes the time, it is all the water in the undercoat behind it. The colour tells you: one even pale pink everywhere, no dark patches.

Can you do one wall and skim the others?

Yes, and plenty of rooms are exactly that: one bad wall off and back, three sound ones skimmed. It is cheaper than doing the lot and it is what the walls are asking for.

What about the electrics and the pipes?

Anything going into the wall goes in before we plaster, not after. Chased cables, moved sockets, first fix pipework, radiator brackets off. Once the wall is finished, cutting into it means a patch, and a patch can show.

Water off, electrics off, everybody out of that room

Wet plaster is heavy and it comes down in pieces rather than politely. Turn the water off at the stopcock if it is still running, switch that room off at the consumer unit, because a wet light fitting is the real danger, get everybody out from underneath and shut the door on it.

What to do now

  1. Find the stopcock and turn the water off. If it is coming from a bathroom or a tank above, that stops it getting worse while you work out the rest.
  2. Turn the electrics off to that room at the consumer unit. Do not touch a light switch, a pendant or a downlight that has water near it.
  3. Get everything out from underneath it, then keep people and pets out and close the door.
  4. Do not stand under a bulge and poke it to let the water out. If plaster is going to come down it will come down on whoever is holding the broom.
  5. Put a bucket and a sheet down if water is still dripping, and take a photograph from the doorway. It helps us and it helps your insurer.

Two jobs, in this order

First, stop the water and find out where it came from, which is normally a plumber or a roofer rather than us. Second, the ceiling. There is no point boarding and skimming anything while it is still getting wet, and any plasterer who offers to is doing you no favours.
