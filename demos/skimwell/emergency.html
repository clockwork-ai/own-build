Has a ceiling come down?

Plastering is not a midnight trade and we are not going to pretend it is. There is one thing in it that genuinely cannot wait, and that is a ceiling that has come down or is about to, usually after a leak. Here is what to do in the first ten minutes, and what the order of jobs is after that.

First ten minutes

Water off, electrics off, nobody underneath.

Do this now

  1. Turn the water off at the stopcock. If it is coming from a bathroom, a tank or a radiator above, that stops it getting worse while you sort the rest out.
  2. Turn the electrics off to that room at the consumer unit. A wet light fitting or a downlight full of water is the real danger here, not the plaster.
  3. Get everybody and the pets out of that room, move what you can from underneath, and shut the door on it.
  4. Put a bucket and a sheet down if it is still dripping, but do it from the edge of the room rather than standing under the bulge.
  5. Take a photograph from the doorway. It helps whoever comes, and it helps your insurer if you end up claiming.

Do not do this

  • Do not stand under a bulge and poke it with a broom to let the water out. If it is coming down, it comes down on whoever is holding the broom, and wet plaster is heavy.
  • Do not touch a light switch, a pendant or a socket in that room until the circuit is off.
  • Do not try to prop the ceiling up. A prop against sodden plaster goes through it.
  • Do not start pulling the loose plaster down yourself. Old ceilings can contain things nobody wants in the air, and the rest of it may be holding on by very little.
  • Do not let anybody board and skim it while whatever leaked is still leaking. That is money straight back on the floor.

The three we get rung about

What each one actually means.

A ceiling down after a leak

Water has soaked the plaster, it has got heavy, and the key or the fixings have let go. Nothing about this one is a plastering emergency in the sense of needing a plasterer tonight. It needs the water off, the electrics off and the room shut. The plastering happens after the leak is fixed and the ceiling has dried, and anybody offering to board it over on the same day is not doing you a favour.

A bulge or a sag, nothing down yet

A ceiling that is bowing or that moves when you press it has lost some of its grip and is carrying weight it should not. Treat it like it is coming down: stay out from under it, keep the room shut, and get it looked at this week rather than next month. If it is wet, the water is the first job. If it is dry and old, it is lath and plaster letting go, which is serious but not tonight.

Plaster off a wall, suddenly

A section of wall plaster coming away in one go, usually with the wallpaper. It looks dramatic and it is almost never urgent, because nothing above anybody's head is involved. Pull the loose bit off if it is already hanging, sweep up, and get the wall looked at. What matters is finding out why, because it is normally damp or a background that was never right.

Two jobs, and the order matters

First, stop the water and find out where it came from. That is a plumber for a pipe, a tank or an overflow, and a roofer for anything coming in from outside. Until that is done and the ceiling has given up its water, there is nothing a plasterer can usefully do. A ceiling boarded over a live leak is money spent twice.

Second, the ceiling itself. Once it is dry, somebody taps it and presses it and decides between three things: seal and skim over a dried stain where the plaster is still gripping, cut out and piece in a section, or take the whole ceiling down and reboard it. On an older lath and plaster ceiling where a decent area has already let go, taking the lot down is normally the right answer, because the rest of the key has aged the same way.

Why we will not rush it

Plaster needs its background to be dry and sound. Skimming a ceiling that is still holding water gives you a lovely flat finish that stains, blisters and lets go within months, and by then everybody has forgotten the leak and blames the plastering. So the honest answer on the phone is often that we will come when it is dry, and the thing worth doing today is the water, the electrics and the door.

Rented houses

If you rent, a ceiling coming down is your landlord's or your agent's job and the repair is not yours to arrange. Tell them in writing, in a message or an email rather than only a phone call, so there is a record of when you said it and what you said. Photographs with it. If somebody could be hurt, say that too, and say which room is now unusable.

Questions

Asked at the worst moment.

There is a bulge in my ceiling with water in it. Do I poke it?

Not while you are stood under it. Wet plaster is heavy and a bulge full of water is holding a lot more than it looks. If it has to be released so the water does not spread, that is somebody doing it from the side of the room with the floor sheeted, not somebody directly underneath with a broom.

What matters more is the water and the electrics. Stopcock off, that room off at the consumer unit, door shut, everybody out. Then the leak, then the ceiling.

How do I know if the whole ceiling has to come down?

Tap it and press it gently, well away from the sagging part. If the rest of it feels firm and sounds solid, a section can often be cut out and pieced in. If it moves, sounds hollow over a wide area or you can see a general bow across the room, the key has gone and the whole thing is usually better down.

On a lath and plaster ceiling where one area has already let go, the rest is the same age and has been through the same hundred years. Taking it down and reboarding is more mess once, instead of patching every few years.

Can you come and board it over today?

On this demo nobody is coming, because the firm is not real and the number rings nobody. On a real site the honest answer is usually no, and for a good reason: a ceiling cannot be boarded and skimmed while whatever leaked is still leaking or while the structure above it is soaked.

What a real firm should do today is tell you how to make it safe, tell you who you actually need first, which is normally a plumber or a roofer, and book to come and look once it is dry.

Is the plaster dust dangerous?

Ordinary gypsum plaster dust is a nuisance rather than a hazard, though nobody wants to breathe it. The thing to be careful about is older material: artex coatings from before the year two thousand can contain asbestos, and so can some old textured coatings and boards.

That is why nobody should be pulling an old ceiling down or sanding old textured coating on a guess. If there is any doubt about what is up there, it gets tested first, and if it is positive the removal is a licensed firm's job rather than a plasterer's.

My insurer is involved. What do they want?

Photographs taken early, including the stained or collapsed area and whatever leaked, and a note of the date and what you did. Keep anything you take off the ceiling until they say otherwise, and do not throw the evidence of the leak away.

Our side of it is a written quote that says what is being done and what is not, which is what an insurer will ask for. There is nothing to claim on this demo, because nothing here is real.
