Coving, done so it disappears

Plain coving is the simple curve where the wall meets the ceiling. It goes up in lengths on adhesive, and the whole job is in the joints and the mitres: cut tight, bedded properly, filled, then rubbed back so the eye runs along the run without catching anywhere. Badly done coving is obvious from the doorway, because every joint shows as a line and every internal corner has a gap filled with a thumb of adhesive.

It is also worth saying what coving is for in an older room. It is not decoration for its own sake. The junction between a wall and a ceiling is where two different things meet and move at slightly different rates, so it is where cracks appear. A cove bridges that junction and hides the movement. That is why so many houses have one.

Ornate cornice, matched rather than replaced

In a Victorian or Edwardian house the cornice is run plaster, often a deep moulding with real detail in it, and it is one of the few things in a room that cannot be bought off a shelf and look right. Where a section has been damaged, by a leak, by a wall coming out, or by somebody fitting a light badly, it gets matched. Either a template is cut to the profile and the new section is run in place by hand, or a mould is taken off a sound length round the corner and the piece is cast and fitted.

Two honest things about it. First, it is slow work and it costs accordingly, because it is a craft job rather than a lengths and adhesive job. Second, a lot of old cornice is choked with ten layers of paint and getting the detail back out of it is careful work in its own right, sometimes more than running the new bit. A plain plastic cove next to a hundred year old run looks exactly like what it is, so matching matters more than people expect.

Ceiling roses and the rest

Roses, corbels, arch mouldings and picture rails all come up in the same houses and all follow the same rule: match what is there, and if it cannot be matched, say so rather than fitting something that nearly does. Anything that involves taking a light fitting down or putting one back is an electrician's bit, not ours, and we will say when a job needs one.

On the job

How this one usually goes.

  1. Look at what is thereThe profile, how many coats of paint are on it, whether the ceiling and wall are flat enough to take a cove, and whether a damaged section can be matched.
  2. Prepare the junctionPaper off, loose paint off, and the line scored where the cove is going so the adhesive has something to grip rather than sitting on emulsion.
  3. Cut and fit, mitres firstInternals and externals cut and offered up dry before anything goes on, so the corners are right rather than filled.
  4. Run or cast any missing detailA template run in place for a plain profile, or a cast taken off a sound length for an ornate one, then let go off before it is fitted.
  5. Fill, rub back and clear upJoints and mitres filled, rubbed back so the run reads as one piece, then the dust off and the offcuts away with us.

A sample running order for this demo. Yours depends on the house and what the walls turn out to be doing.

Questions

Asked about this job.

Can you match my old cornice?

Usually, yes. Either a template is cut to the profile and the new piece is run in place, or a mould is taken off a sound length elsewhere in the room and the section is cast. Where it genuinely cannot be matched we will say that rather than fit something that nearly does.

Is coving worth putting up?

It covers the one junction in a room that moves, which is where cracks turn up, so it earns its place as well as looking right. In a modern room with a crisp square line, plenty of people prefer no cove at all. Both are fine, it is a choice rather than a fault.

Do you do plaster or the lightweight stuff?

Both. Plaster coving is heavier, takes a knock better and feels right in an older house. Lightweight is easier on a long run and on a ceiling that is not perfectly flat. In an older room with original detail, plaster every time.

My cornice is thick with paint. Can the detail come back?

Often, but it is careful slow work and it has to be done without damaging what is underneath. On a badly choked run it is sometimes better to cast a new section from a cleaner length than to try to strip the old one back.

Can you fit a ceiling rose?

We can fit and make good the plaster side of it. The electrical connection is an electrician's job and we would not touch it, which is worth planning in before we come rather than after.

Water off, electrics off, everybody out of that room

Wet plaster is heavy and it comes down in pieces rather than politely. Turn the water off at the stopcock if it is still running, switch that room off at the consumer unit, because a wet light fitting is the real danger, get everybody out from underneath and shut the door on it.

What to do now

  1. Find the stopcock and turn the water off. If it is coming from a bathroom or a tank above, that stops it getting worse while you work out the rest.
  2. Turn the electrics off to that room at the consumer unit. Do not touch a light switch, a pendant or a downlight that has water near it.
  3. Get everything out from underneath it, then keep people and pets out and close the door.
  4. Do not stand under a bulge and poke it to let the water out. If plaster is going to come down it will come down on whoever is holding the broom.
  5. Put a bucket and a sheet down if water is still dripping, and take a photograph from the doorway. It helps us and it helps your insurer.

Two jobs, in this order

First, stop the water and find out where it came from, which is normally a plumber or a roofer rather than us. Second, the ceiling. There is no point boarding and skimming anything while it is still getting wet, and any plasterer who offers to is doing you no favours.
