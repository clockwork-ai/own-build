Rendering

Rendering.

Sand and cement in two coats, or a modern silicone thin coat that comes coloured all the way through. What the wall underneath is doing decides which of them is right.

Get a quote

The two kinds, honestly

Sand and cement is the traditional one: a scratch coat, then a floating coat, worked with a wooden float so it keeps an open grain. It is strong, it has been on houses round here for eighty years, and it takes masonry paint well. What it does not do is move, so on a wall that flexes it cracks, and once water is behind it, it blows off in patches.

Silicone thin coat, sometimes called a through coloured render, goes on much thinner over a reinforced basecoat with mesh in it. The colour is in the material rather than on it, so there is nothing to repaint in five years. It is flexible, it sheds dirt and water well, and it stays looking even. It costs more and it is less forgiving of a bad background, because there is no thickness to hide anything in.

Pebbledash, and why patching it never works

Pebbledash is stone thrown into a wet bed of render. It was put on half the houses round here because it is hard wearing and it hides everything underneath. The problem is what happens when it fails: water gets behind it, a whole sheet lets go and comes off like a plate, and the patch you put back never matches, because the stone, the bed colour and the age are all different. On a shady elevation it also holds dirt and goes green.

So the answer with a failed pebbledash wall is nearly always the whole elevation rather than a patch, and that is the moment people choose whether to go back with dash or move to a thin coat. Both are honest answers. What is not honest is somebody dashing over a patch and telling you it will blend in.

The background comes first

Every one of these finishes is only as sound as the wall it is on. Before anything goes on, the existing render gets tapped over for hollow areas, the beads and the bottom edge get looked at, the ground levels get a mention if they are above the damp course, and anything damp gets traced rather than covered. Render put over a loose background comes off with it, and the finish gets the blame.

Weather matters too. Render does not want to go on in frost, in driving rain or in hard summer sun, because all three ruin the way it cures. Three to four days with the scaffold up for an elevation, and more if the week turns.

On the job

How this one usually goes.

  1. Scaffold or tower, and the wall tappedAccess up properly rather than reaching off a ladder. Then the existing render tapped over for hollow patches and the bottom edge checked.
  2. Take off what has let goBlown render comes off back to the masonry. If that turns out to be most of the elevation, better to know on day one than halfway through.
  3. Beads and stopsStop beads at the edges, a bell or drip bead at the bottom so water leaves the wall rather than running behind it, and movement beads where the wall needs them.
  4. Basecoat, and mesh where it belongsScratch coat on a sand and cement job, or a reinforced basecoat with mesh in it under a thin coat. Flattened and left to set.
  5. Finish coat and clear upFloated sand and cement, or the through coloured thin coat worked to an even texture. Then the scaffold down, splashes off the paths and windows, and the waste away.

Questions

Asked about this job.

Render or pebbledash, which is best?

It depends on the wall, not the look. Pebbledash is tough and hides a lot but cannot be patched invisibly and goes green in the shade. A silicone thin coat is coloured through so it needs no repainting, sheds dirt and moves a little. Sand and cement is still right on plenty of older solid walls and takes paint. The background decides more than the finish does.

My render is cracked. Does it all come off?

Not always. Fine surface crazing can often stay and be coated over. A crack you can get a nail into, or an area that sounds hollow when tapped, has let go and has to come off. We tap the whole elevation before saying either way.

Will rendering cure my damp?

No, and anyone who says it will is selling you something. Render can stop driving rain soaking a porous wall, which helps. It does nothing for rising damp, a leak, a blocked gully, high ground levels or condensation, and sealing a wall that needs to breathe can make things worse.

Can you render over pebbledash?

Sometimes, if it is sound and well keyed, and it needs the stones knocking back and a proper basecoat over the lot. If any of it is drummy it has to come off first, because the new render will only be as stuck down as what is under it.

What time of year is best?

Spring and autumn. Not frost, not driving rain, not beating sun. All three affect the cure and show in the finish, and we would rather put a week back than lay render in the wrong weather.

Water off, electrics off, everybody out of that room

Wet plaster is heavy and it comes down in pieces rather than politely. Turn the water off at the stopcock if it is still running, switch that room off at the consumer unit, because a wet light fitting is the real danger, get everybody out from underneath and shut the door on it.

What to do now

  1. Find the stopcock and turn the water off. If it is coming from a bathroom or a tank above, that stops it getting worse while you work out the rest.
  2. Turn the electrics off to that room at the consumer unit. Do not touch a light switch, a pendant or a downlight that has water near it.
  3. Get everything out from underneath it, then keep people and pets out and close the door.
  4. Do not stand under a bulge and poke it to let the water out. If plaster is going to come down it will come down on whoever is holding the broom.
  5. Put a bucket and a sheet down if water is still dripping, and take a photograph from the doorway. It helps us and it helps your insurer.

Two jobs, in this order

First, stop the water and find out where it came from, which is normally a plumber or a roofer rather than us. Second, the ceiling. There is no point boarding and skimming anything while it is still getting wet, and any plasterer who offers to is doing you no favours.
