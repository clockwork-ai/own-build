Planning a bathroom refit: the order things happen

Every refit runs in roughly the same order. Knowing it is what stops you buying the wrong thing at the wrong time.

Bathroom jobs go wrong in predictable places, and most of them come down to order: something bought too late, a trade booked too early, or a decision made after the point where it could still be made cheaply. A refit runs in roughly the same order every time. Knowing it makes you a much easier customer to look after, and it saves you money.

Before anybody starts

Decide the layout before the tiles

The single biggest cost decision is whether the toilet moves. Everything else can be shuffled fairly cheaply, because taps and wastes for a bath or a basin have some give in them. The toilet has to fall to the soil pipe, so moving it means lifting the floor and sometimes going through a wall. Settle that first. Tile choice can wait.

Get everything on site and open the boxes

The day the old suite comes out is not the day to find the basin is cracked or the waste is missing. Have it all delivered, unpacked and checked a week before. A missing part at first fix stops the whole job, because the next five steps queue behind it.

Order about ten per cent extra tiles

For cuts, breakages and the repair you will want in three years. Keep the spares. The same batch will not match later.

The order on site

  1. Strip outSuite, tiles and flooring out, and the first honest look at what is behind them. Expect something: a soft board under the bath, blown plaster, a joint somebody bodged. This is when it gets found and agreed, not later.
  2. First fix plumbingWater and waste runs moved to suit the new layout, pipes clipped properly and pressure tested before anything covers them. Any wall hung frame gets built in now.
  3. First fix electricsCables run for the extractor, the lighting and any shaver socket or electric shower, by a qualified electrician. It has to happen before the walls close up.
  4. Boarding, floor and making goodWalls boarded or made good, the floor levelled, and in a wet room the former set and the whole area tanked and left to go off properly.
  5. TilingSet out dry from a centre line first so the cuts land in corners, then fixed, then left to set before grouting. Corners sealed rather than grouted.
  6. Second fix and fittingSuite, taps, screen, towel rail and the extractor connected. The toilet goes back for good here if it has been on a temporary connection.
  7. Test, seal and hand overEverything run with the water on and watched, the last sealant lines in, the room cleaned down, and a walk round with you before anybody calls it finished.

How long it takes

A straightforward full refit in an average British bathroom is usually around one to two weeks on site. A shower room or an en suite is normally quicker. What stretches it is moving the toilet, bad floorboards, plaster that has to come off and go back, and tiling that needs setting out round a window. A quote that says how long yours should take is worth more than one that does not.

The toilet question

In a one bathroom house this is the thing people worry about most, and rightly. A fitter who is thinking about it will take the old toilet out in the morning and put it back on a temporary connection before leaving, so you have a working toilet overnight. There are usually a day or two in the middle, while the floor goes down or the soil connection moves, when it cannot go back. Ask which days those are before you start, not on the morning.

What to ask on the first visit

  • Is the toilet moving, and what does that add?
  • Who is doing the electrical work, and when do they come?
  • Which days will we not have a toilet?
  • What happens to the price if you find something behind the tiles?
  • Are the corners being sealed or grouted?
  • How long between the last tile and the first use of the shower?

None of those are awkward questions. Anybody who has done a few bathrooms will have an answer ready, and the answers tell you plenty about how the job will run.

Written for this demo as general guidance. It is not advice on your bathroom, and products and rules change. Check anything that matters with somebody who has seen the room.

Isolate the water first, then ring

Stop the water before you do anything else. Turn the shower or the tap off at the valve, and if it keeps coming, shut the stopcock, usually under the kitchen sink. Then ring and tell us what you have got and we will say honestly whether it needs somebody tonight or in the morning.

While you wait

  1. Nobody uses the shower, the bath or that toilet until we have seen it.
  2. Leave the lights and any socket near the wet patch alone. Switch that circuit off at the board if you can do it safely.
  3. Put a bowl under the drip and towels round it. If the ceiling is bulging, do not stand under it.
  4. Move anything that matters out from underneath, and lift the carpet edge if it is soaking.
  5. Take a couple of photos of the stain and of the bathroom floor. They help us work out where it is coming from.

If water is near a light fitting or a consumer unit and you are not sure, turn the power off and call an electrician or 999 before you call us.
