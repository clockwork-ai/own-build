Why internal doors stick, and what actually fixes it.

Four causes, and only one of them is the door. How to tell which one you have before anybody takes a plane to it.

Generated for this demo. Not a photograph of a real job.

Find the cause before you take anything off

A door that rubs is one of the few jobs where the obvious fix is usually the wrong one. Plane the door, and if the real problem was a loose hinge or a settling frame, you now have a door with a gap round it and the rubbing comes back anyway. So the first job is working out which of four things it is, and that takes five minutes with your eyes and a hand.

One: the timber has swollen

The commonest by far, and the easiest to spot: it rubs in wet weather and goes away in a dry spell. Timber takes up moisture from the air and swells across its width, and a door hung a shade tight in a dry February will catch in a wet August.

What fixes it is patience, and sometimes paint. A door sealed properly on all six faces, including the top and bottom edges which are nearly always left bare, takes up far less water. If it has to be eased, it gets eased in the wet season when it is at its biggest, not in the dry when it is at its smallest.

Two: a hinge has worked loose

Open the door halfway and lift the handle edge gently. If there is play, or you can see the top hinge leaf standing away from the frame, that is your answer. A heavy door hanging on screws that have pulled in soft or painted timber lets the whole door drop, and then it catches at the top on the lock side.

The fix is longer screws into sound timber, or, where the screw holes have gone, drilling them out and gluing in timber plugs so there is fresh wood to screw into. That is a twenty minute job that stops a door rubbing for another twenty years, and it is the one people most often plane instead.

Three: the frame or the floor has moved

Look at the gap all the way round with the door closed. If it tapers, something is out of square. Houses settle, floors sag towards the middle of a room, and a doorway in an old house is rarely still the shape it was built as.

Here easing the door is the right answer, but it has to follow the shape the opening has now rather than a straight line. The door gets held up, scribed to the actual opening, taken down in the right place and rehung. Where the lining itself has twisted, the lining is the thing to fix.

Four: something else changed

New carpet is the big one, especially with a thick underlay: the door that cleared the old floor by a few millimetres now drags. Thick paint built up in the rebate is another, and so is a latch that no longer lines up with its keep, which feels exactly like a sticking door but is not.

Each has its own fix and none of them is planing the whole door. Carpet means taking a little off the bottom, which is the one edge where a generous gap does no harm. Paint means cutting it out of the rebate. A latch out of line means moving the keep a couple of millimetres.

How to tell which one you have, in five minutes

Close the door and look at the gap all round. Even gap that has simply got tighter, and it is wet outside: swollen timber. Gap tapering so it is tight at the top on the lock side: dropped hinge. Gap tapering the other way or tight at one corner only: frame or floor movement. Door clears everywhere but will not latch: the keep. Door drags on the floor only: whatever is on the floor.

Then look at where the paint is worn or shiny on the edge of the door, because that marks exactly where it is rubbing. That, and the weather, tells you nearly everything.

What a joiner would do differently

Check the hinges before touching the door. Ease in the wet season, not the dry. Take material off the hinge edge rather than the lock edge where there is a choice, because it is easier to make good. Seal every face afterwards, including the top and bottom. And never plane a door the first time it rubs, because a door that rubs in one week of bad weather each year is a door that fits.

Head and sidesAn even gap of around two millimetres round the top and both sides, the same all the way along. Even matters more than exact: a gap that tapers is the thing your eye picks up from across a landing, long before it notices the size of it.
Hinge sideThe hinge knuckles decide this one. Recesses cut so the leaves sit flush with the timber, not proud and not sunk, and the screws central in their holes. A hinge sunk too deep is what makes a door spring back off the frame.
At the floorMore at the bottom, usually six millimetres or so, and more again over carpet. It is not slackness, it is how air gets round a house, and it is what stops the door catching the moment the carpet is fitted.
The one people forgetTimber moves with the weather. A door fitted dead tight in a dry February will bind in a wet August, so a joiner leaves it a shade generous on purpose and does not plane a door the first time it rubs.

This is the one thing we would drop a job for

A front or back door that will not shut properly is not a tidy up job for next week. It is a house that cannot be locked tonight. Same with a door hanging off its hinges, a frame split round the lock, or a door that has been forced and now fouls the frame.

What a call gets you

  1. A few questions on the phone, so we know whether this is joinery, a lock or glass before anybody sets off.
  2. The door made to shut and lock if the timber can take it, which it usually can.
  3. Boarding or a temporary fixing where the door or the frame has to be ordered.
  4. A straight answer about what wants doing properly afterwards, and what can wait.

What we will not do

Print a response time. There is no number on this site for how fast anybody turns up, because any number we put here would be made up. Ring and you will be told honestly when somebody can be there.

If the door shuts but the lock or the key is the problem, that is a locksmith rather than a joiner. If there is broken glass in the door, get that made safe first.
