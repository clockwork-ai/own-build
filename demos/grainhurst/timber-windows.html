Sash and timber window repairs

Sash and timber window repairs.

Rotten sections spliced out and new timber let in, sash cords and weights sorted, and the whole thing working again. Usually a fraction of a new window, and the original stays in the house.

Get a quote

How much of the window is actually gone

Far less than people think, nearly always. Water gets into timber at the joints and at the bottom, so the decay is concentrated in the bottom rail of the sash, the sill, and the bottom few inches of the stiles. The top two thirds of the window is often perfectly sound timber that has been there a century and will be there another one.

The way to find out is a bradawl or a screwdriver, pushed gently into the timber in a dozen places. Sound timber stops it dead. Soft timber lets it sink in, and that tells you exactly where the repair starts and stops. It takes ten minutes and it is the difference between a repair and a replacement.

What splicing in new timber means

The soft section gets cut out back to sound timber, square and clean, and a new piece of the same sort of timber is cut to fit the hole and glued and pinned in. On a sill or a bottom rail that might be a long shallow piece. On the bottom of a stile it is often a short splice with a long angled joint so the glue line does not sit where water runs.

Done properly the repair is stronger than the surrounding old timber and invisible under paint. What it is not is a tub of filler pressed into a soft hole, which is what is usually under the paint when somebody tells you their windows are beyond saving. Filler has no strength, it does not hold a screw and it traps water behind it.

Sashes that will not slide

Three things stop a sash window working. The cord has snapped, so the weight is sitting in the bottom of the box and the sash either will not stay up or will not move. Paint has built up over decades in the channels and glued everything together. Or the staff bead is screwed on too tight against the sash.

None of those is a broken window. The cord is replaced through the pocket at the side of the box frame, over the pulley, tied to the same weights that were always in there. The paint gets cut out of the channels. The beads get adjusted. Then it slides, it stays where you put it, and it stops rattling in the wind. Draught seals can go in at the same time, which is the one thing that makes an old sash behave more like a new window without replacing it.

On the job

How this one usually goes.

  1. The awl test, all roundTimber pushed in a dozen places on each window: the sill, the bottom rail, the bottom of each stile, the joints and the beads. What is soft gets marked, and that is the honest size of the job.
  2. You get told what is soundWhich parts are being kept, which are being spliced, and which really are past it. If a whole sash wants making new, we will say so rather than patch it twice.
  3. Soft timber cut outBack to sound timber, square and clean, with the joint arranged so water runs off the repair rather than into it.
  4. New timber let inSame sort of timber, cut to fit dry, then glued and pinned. Trimmed and planed back flush so the moulding runs through as if nothing happened.
  5. Cords, weights and beadsCords replaced through the pockets and tied back onto the original weights, pulleys freed, the paint build up cut out of the channels, and the beads reset so the sash slides without rattling.
  6. Ready for paint, and workingPrimed on the repair, sashes slid up and down, checked that they stay where you put them, and the putty line and the joints left ready for the decorator.

Questions

Asked about this job.

Is it worth repairing, or should I just replace?

Start by finding out how much is actually gone, because the answer is usually a lot less than it looks. The decay is concentrated at the bottom and in the joints, and the rest is often sound.

A repair keeps the original window, which in an older stone house is a big part of how the front of the house looks, and it costs a fraction of a replacement. Where we will tell you to replace is when the sashes themselves are shot through, when the box frame has gone at the back where you cannot see it, or when somebody has filled it so thoroughly that there is nothing left to repair.

Are you allowed to change windows in a listed building or a conservation area?

That is not a joinery question, it is a planning one, and it is worth asking the council before anybody quotes you. Rules differ between a listed building and a conservation area, and between one council and the next.

What is usually true is that repairing an original window is the least complicated option of all. We will do the joinery. The permissions are yours to get and we would rather say that at the start than halfway through.

Can old sash windows be draught proofed?

Yes, and it is the single best thing you can do to an old sash short of replacing it. Brush seals go into the channels and against the meeting rail, and the rattle and most of the draught go with them.

It works best done at the same time as the repair, because the sashes are out anyway. Done on its own it still helps a lot. Nobody should tell you it makes an old window the same as a new one, but the difference you feel sitting next to it is real.

What about secondary glazing or new glass?

Glass is a glazier's job and we work alongside one rather than pretend otherwise. If a pane is cracked or you want a thin double glazed unit in an old sash, that is worth a conversation early because the unit's thickness changes the rebate and the weight of the sash, and the weights may need balancing to suit.

Secondary glazing is joinery and we can fit it. It is less pretty than a repaired sash and it is very good at noise.

How long does the paint last on a repaired window?

Longer on the repair than on the rest of it, usually, because new timber properly primed on all faces is in better shape than old timber that has been painted over fifteen times.

There is no number on this page because it depends on which way the window faces, how exposed it is and how well it was painted. A sheltered north window and a south west facing one in a Pennine valley are not the same thing at all.

This is the one thing we would drop a job for

A front or back door that will not shut properly is not a tidy up job for next week. It is a house that cannot be locked tonight. Same with a door hanging off its hinges, a frame split round the lock, or a door that has been forced and now fouls the frame.

What a call gets you

  1. A few questions on the phone, so we know whether this is joinery, a lock or glass before anybody sets off.
  2. The door made to shut and lock if the timber can take it, which it usually can.
  3. Boarding or a temporary fixing where the door or the frame has to be ordered.
  4. A straight answer about what wants doing properly afterwards, and what can wait.

What we will not do

Print a response time. There is no number on this site for how fast anybody turns up, because any number we put here would be made up. Ring and you will be told honestly when somebody can be there.

If the door shuts but the lock or the key is the problem, that is a locksmith rather than a joiner. If there is broken glass in the door, get that made safe first.
