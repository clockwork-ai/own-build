Looking after a new driveway.

What to do in the first year, what to leave alone, and the three jobs that keep a drive looking like it did on the last day.

Generated for this demo. Not a photograph of a real job.

The first few weeks

A new drive is not fragile, but it is new, and a few things are worth knowing. Block paving settles very slightly as the joints take up, so a top up of joint sand after the first few weeks is normal rather than a sign of anything wrong. Tarmac is soft for a while in warm weather and will mark under a point load. Resin and mortar joints want their curing time before anything heavy sits on them.

So for the first month: keep skip legs, jack stands, bike stands and trailer jockey wheels off it, or put a board under them. Do not turn the steering wheel hard while stationary on brand new tarmac in hot sun. Keep diesel and oil off everything, and if something does spill, soak it up rather than hose it along the drive.

The three jobs that matter

  • Keep the joints full. On block paving the joint sand is not decoration, it is what stops the blocks moving. Top it up when it looks low, particularly in the first year and after any wash. A bag of kiln dried sand and a stiff brush on a dry day is the whole job.
  • Brush it. Half of what makes a drive look old is the thin layer of grit, leaves and moss that sits on it. Brushing it a few times a year does more for the look of a drive than washing it once every five.
  • Keep the water moving. Clear the channel drain grating, the gully and the bottom of the downpipe. A blocked gully sends water under the paving instead of into the drain, and water under paving is what causes the dips people ring us about years later.

Washing, without wrecking it

A rotary head on a pressure washer, kept moving, held at a sensible distance. Not a pencil jet and not held in one spot. A pencil jet cuts the face off a concrete block and leaves a stripe you will see from the road for the next ten years, and it blasts the joint sand out of everything.

Then re-sand it. Washing takes the joint sand out whether you meant it to or not, and a washed drive that is not re-sanded will start moving within a year. Kiln dried sand, brushed in, left to settle, brushed again. If somebody washes a block drive for you and does not re-sand it, they have done half a job.

Weeds, moss and the stuff in the joints

Weeds in paving grow from seeds that land on top and settle into a low joint. They are not coming up through the sub base. So the answer is a full joint and a brush, not drastic chemicals. Moss is the same conversation: it takes hold where the sand is low and the surface stays damp, which is usually the shady side and the bottom of a fall.

On flags it is the pointing rather than sand, and a joint that keeps failing is telling you something. Usually that the flag under it is on dabs rather than a full bed, and it is flexing every time somebody stands on it.

Sealing: optional, and worth a winter's wait

Sealing a block drive holds the joint sand, slows oil marks and makes the next wash easier. It is not required and plenty of good drives never see any. The things to know: it wants a clean, properly dry drive, it needs doing again every few years, and a seal applied over damp goes cloudy and looks worse than nothing.

So let a new drive have its first winter, see how you feel about it, then decide in a dry spell. A firm offering to wash and seal in the same day in November is offering you something that will not work.

When to ring somebody

A single dip appearing where you park, a flag that has started to rock, a channel drain that holds water after the rain has stopped, or a joint that keeps opening however much sand goes in. All four are small jobs caught early and bigger ones left. None of them means a new drive.

Sunken paving, or something somebody could trip on?

This is not an emergency trade, and we will not pretend it is

Nobody is coming out at midnight to lay blocks. What we will do is get a trip hazard made safe quickly, then book the proper repair in daylight.

Make it safe first

  1. Mark it so people can see it. A cone, a bucket, a chair, a bag of sand, anything that stops somebody walking into it in the dark.
  2. If a flag is rocking, lift it right out rather than leaving it to tip under somebody's foot, and stand it against the wall.
  3. Keep the route to the front door clear, even if that means going round the long way for a few days.
  4. Take a photograph with something in it for scale, a shoe or a tape, before anything is moved.

If it is on the pavement rather than your drive

A lifted or broken footway flag outside your boundary belongs to the council, and it is their job to repair it. Report it to them. Do not pay anybody to mend the public footway.

Making safe is not the repair. A sunken patch is lifted, the layer underneath is put right and the same blocks or flags go back down. That gets booked properly rather than rushed in the dark.
