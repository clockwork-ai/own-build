First ten minutes

Do and do not.

Do

  • Ring 999 first if somebody has already fallen and is hurt. The paving can wait as long as you like.
  • Mark it so it can be seen in the dark. A cone, a bin, a chair, a bag of sand, anything that breaks the line somebody walks.
  • Lift a flag that rocks right out and stand it against the wall. A loose flag that tips under a heel is worse than an obvious hole.
  • Keep one clear route to the front door, even if it is the long way round for a few days.
  • Photograph it with a shoe or a tape in shot for scale, before anything is moved.
  • Tell anybody who visits regularly, and anybody delivering, that the path has a lip in it.

Do not

  • Do not wedge a sunken flag up with stones or a bit of timber and call it done. It will move again, usually under somebody.
  • Do not pour concrete or quick setting mix into the gap to level it. It traps the water that caused the problem and makes the proper repair twice the work.
  • Do not repair the public footway outside your boundary. That belongs to the council and it is their job.
  • Do not leave a hole open and unlit overnight where a stranger walks, particularly near a bin store or a letterbox.
  • Do not jet wash round the edges of a sunken patch to make it look tidier. You will wash more of the bed out.

Two common ones

What happens when you ring.

A patch of drive that has sunk

Usually by the garage threshold or exactly where the wheels park. We lift the blocks, find whether it is a dip in the laying course or something soft deeper down, put that right and relay the same blocks. Until then the answer is cones and a wide berth, because a dip that holds water becomes a sheet of ice in the first frost.

A flag or a kerb edge that is a trip hazard

A tipped flag, a lifted path edge, a kerb stone that has rolled forward. The quick job is to take the loose one out so nothing can tip, make the lip obvious and keep the route clear. The repair is a full bed and a proper joint, in daylight, once we know whether the bed washed out or was never there.

What making safe actually means

It means nobody can trip on it tonight. The loose flag out rather than balanced, the lip marked so it is obvious in the dark, the route to the door clear, and anything open covered with something that will take a foot.

What it does not mean is mended. A wedge, a shovel of sand or a bag of postmix tipped into a hole is not a repair, and all three make the real one harder. The repair is lifting it, finding out why it sank and putting that right, which is a daylight job with the right gear on the van.

Whose paving is it

Inside your boundary it is yours, which includes the drive, the path to your door and usually the bit of path to a shared bin store. The footway along the street, the kerb and the crossing over it belong to the council, even the part directly outside your gate.

So if a footway flag has lifted or a kerb has broken, report it to the council rather than paying anybody to mend it. Most have a form or a phone line for exactly this, they keep a record of when it was reported, and that record matters if somebody does fall. If you are not sure where the boundary is, your deeds or the council will tell you.

If somebody has already fallen

Look after the person first. If they are hurt, ring 999, and do not move them to tidy up. Once everybody is alright, photograph the paving exactly as it is, with something in shot for scale, before anything is changed.

If it happened on the public footway, report it to the council the same day and keep the reference. If it happened on your own property and somebody else was hurt, tell your insurer before you start putting it right, because they will want to see it as it was.

Rented houses

Ring the landlord or the agent before you book anybody. In most tenancies they arrange and pay for repairs to the paths and the drive, and getting work done without telling them starts a different argument. Making a trip hazard safe is different: cone it, mark it, take the loose flag out, and tell them the same day in writing.

Questions

Asked at the worst moment.

A flag on my path has sunk and my mother nearly went over. What now?

Take the loose flag out if it rocks, so nothing can tip, and mark the gap with something somebody will see in the dark. Keep one clear route to the door. Then book the repair in daylight.

The repair is not a wedge. The flag comes up, we find out whether the bed washed out, was laid on dabs or was never there, and it goes back on a full bed with the joint pointed. That is a short job and it stays put.

Can you come out tonight?

Paving is not an emergency trade and a drive is not a burst pipe, so we will not promise you a van in the dark. What we will do is tell you on the phone how to make it safe in ten minutes, and get the repair booked quickly. Anybody promising you a midnight paving crew is selling you something.

The broken flag is on the pavement outside my house. Is that mine?

Almost certainly not. The footway, the kerb and the crossing over it belong to the council, including the length right outside your gate. Report it to them, keep the reference, and do not pay anybody to repair the public footway.

My drive has sunk in one spot. Does the whole thing need doing again?

Usually not. One dip is usually one patch: blocks up, the cause found and put right, same blocks back down. The time it means more is when the same fault shows in several places at once, because that points at the base rather than at a patch, and we would tell you that rather than relay the same spot twice.

There is a hole where a manhole or a gully has collapsed. Is that a paving job?

Not on its own. A collapsed gully, a broken manhole or a sunken cover over a drain is a drainage job first, and if it is a sewer it may not even be yours to mend. Cover it so nobody can step in it, keep everybody off it, and ring your water company or the council before you ring a paving firm.

Need somebody to look at it?

Tell us what has sunk, what it is doing and your postcode. If somebody is hurt, that comes first and it is 999, not us.
