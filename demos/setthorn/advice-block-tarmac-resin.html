Block paving, tarmac or resin compared.

Three surfaces, three sets of trade offs, and one question underneath all of them that nobody asks until the quotes land.

Generated for this demo. Not a photograph of a real job.

The question underneath the question

People ring up having already decided between three surfaces. The decision that matters more comes first, and it is this: what is underneath, and where is the water going. A good surface on a bad base fails. A sensible surface on a proper base lasts decades. The choice between the three only starts mattering once that is settled.

So read this as what each one is like to live with, rather than which one is best. All three are a reasonable answer to the right drive.

What a drive is made of

The ground underneathDug out, not built onEverything starts with taking out the topsoil, the old concrete or whatever the last firm left. Soft ground gets dug deeper until it stops being soft. This is the layer nobody quotes you for and the one that decides whether the drive is still flat in five winters.
The sub baseThe layer that carries the carCrushed stone, spread in layers and whacked down hard until it stops moving. It spreads the weight of a car over the ground below so no single spot takes it. Thin here and the drive dips exactly where your wheels sit, which is the fault almost everybody who rings us about a sunken drive actually has.
The laying courseThe thin bed on topA shallow layer of sharp sand for blocks, or mortar for flags, screeded level so each unit can be tapped down to the same height. It is thin on purpose. Trying to make up for a bad sub base by piling sand in is the shortcut that leaves ruts.
The surface and the edgesWhat you see, and what holds it inBlocks, flags, tarmac or resin, then the part people forget: an edge, haunched in concrete, running right round. Without it the whole field of paving creeps sideways a few millimetres a year and the joints open up. Edges first, surface after.

Block paving

Concrete blocks or clay pavers, laid dry on a sand bed and locked together by the joints and the edge restraint. Herringbone for a drive, because it holds against a turning wheel.

  • Lives well with: anything that might need digging up later. A cable, a water main, a dip to correct. Blocks come up and go back with nothing to show.
  • Needs: edges haunched right round, and the joints kept full of sand. Those two things are most of why some block drives look tired at eight years and others do not.
  • Gives you trouble when: the sub base was thin. It shows as two dips exactly where you park, and no amount of washing hides it.
  • Ages like: something you maintain. A wash and a re-sand every few years and it looks close to new.

Tarmac

Stone bound with bitumen, laid hot and rolled. One continuous surface, no joints, nothing to re-sand.

  • Lives well with: long drives and large areas, where laying every unit by hand gets expensive. Also with anybody who wants a quiet surface and no weeds.
  • Needs: a firm edge to stop it crumbling away, a dry base to go down on, and a bit of care in its first hot summer.
  • Gives you trouble when: something has to be dug up. The patch will always show slightly, however neatly it is done.
  • Ages like: a road. It greys off from black over the first year or two, which is normal rather than a fault.

Resin bound gravel

Dry stone mixed with a clear resin and trowelled out over a sound base. Looks close to loose gravel, stays where it is put, and water goes through it.

  • Lives well with: drives where you want gravel and not the sweeping up. Also where water has to go through the surface rather than run somewhere.
  • Needs: a genuinely sound base, and the right stone and resin for a drive rather than a path. Cannot be lifted and relaid.
  • Gives you trouble when: it has been laid thin, laid on a moving base, or it is actually resin bonded, which is a scatter coat and a different thing altogether.
  • Ages like: a surface rather than a set of units. Tyre marks and oil are the things to keep on top of, and a small damaged area is a patch.

The honest comparison

If you want to be able to put it right cheaply forever, block paving. If you have a big area and want it quiet and quick, tarmac. If you want the look of gravel and need water to go through, resin bound. If somebody is pushing one of the three hard without having looked at your levels or asked where the water goes, that is the thing to be careful of rather than the material they picked.

And whichever it is, ask what is going underneath. Depth of dig, what sub base, how many layers, how it is compacted, and where the edges are. A firm that answers all five without hesitating is telling you more about the job than the brochure is.

Sunken paving, or something somebody could trip on?

This is not an emergency trade, and we will not pretend it is

Nobody is coming out at midnight to lay blocks. What we will do is get a trip hazard made safe quickly, then book the proper repair in daylight.

Make it safe first

  1. Mark it so people can see it. A cone, a bucket, a chair, a bag of sand, anything that stops somebody walking into it in the dark.
  2. If a flag is rocking, lift it right out rather than leaving it to tip under somebody's foot, and stand it against the wall.
  3. Keep the route to the front door clear, even if that means going round the long way for a few days.
  4. Take a photograph with something in it for scale, a shoe or a tape, before anything is moved.

If it is on the pavement rather than your drive

A lifted or broken footway flag outside your boundary belongs to the council, and it is their job to repair it. Report it to them. Do not pay anybody to mend the public footway.

Making safe is not the repair. A sunken patch is lifted, the layer underneath is put right and the same blocks or flags go back down. That gets booked properly rather than rushed in the dark.
