Tarmac and resin driveways

Tarmac and resin driveways.

Tarmac where you want one quiet surface laid quickly. Resin bound where you want the look of gravel without sweeping it off the pavement every week.

Ask for a quote

They are not the same thing at all

They get grouped together because both arrive as a lorry load and go down as one continuous surface, but they behave differently once they are on. Tarmac is a bound stone and bitumen surface, rolled hot, and it wants a good edge to hold it. Resin bound is dry stone mixed with a clear resin and trowelled out, and it lets water straight through.

What they share is that neither can be lifted and put back. A trench for a cable or a dip that needs correcting is a patch in both cases, and a patch always shows a little. That is the trade off against block paving, and it is worth knowing before you choose rather than after.

When tarmac is the right answer

Long drives, where paving the lot in blocks is a lot of money for a surface you drive over. Drives where you want no joints, no weeds and nothing to re-sand. Anywhere quiet matters, because tarmac is noticeably quieter underfoot and under a wheel than paving.

It wants laying in dry weather on a dry base, it wants an edge, and it wants a few weeks before anything heavy stands on it in hot sun. Power steering turned hard on a brand new tarmac drive in July will scuff it, and that is worth hearing from us rather than finding out.

When resin bound is the right answer

Where the look of gravel is the point but gravel in the road, in the lawn and in the hall is not. Where you need water to go through the surface rather than run somewhere. And where a sound existing base means we can prepare and overlay rather than dig everything out, which is the one case where the job gets genuinely quicker.

Resin bound is not the same as resin bonded, which is resin painted on and stone scattered over the top. Bound is mixed through and trowelled; bonded is a scatter coat. If a quote says resin and you cannot tell which, ask, because they do not last the same.

On the job

How this one usually goes.

  1. We check what is underneathFor an overlay that matters more than anything. Sound, level and no movement, or it comes out. We would rather lose the job than overlay a broken base.
  2. Dig out or prepareA new drive gets dug out and a sub base built. An overlay gets cleaned, any failed patches cut out, and the levels checked against the thickness going on.
  3. Edges and drainageBoth surfaces want something at the edge to stop them crumbling away, and both want the water question answered before the lorry turns up.
  4. Laying day, and it is one dayTarmac goes down hot and gets rolled. Resin is mixed in batches and trowelled out. Either way the drive is a continuous surface by the end of the day and nobody can walk on it for a bit.
  5. Curing, and what not to do yetA few hours before you can walk on it, longer before a car, and a bit of care in the first hot spell. We tell you the actual times on the day rather than printing a number here.

Questions

Asked about this job.

Is tarmac cheaper than block paving?

Usually, on a big area, and the reason is labour rather than material. There is no laying each unit by hand. On a small drive the difference narrows, because the lorry, the plant and the crew have to turn up either way. We will not print figures on a demo site, but we will tell you plainly where the line is for your drive.

Can you lay resin over my old concrete?

Sometimes. Sound, level concrete with no movement and no serious cracking can take a resin overlay. Cracked concrete sat on nothing cannot, because the crack will come straight through. The honest check takes ten minutes on site and it is worth more than any amount of talking about it on the phone.

Does resin bound let water through?

Yes, that is one of the main reasons people choose it, but only if everything under it drains as well. Permeable resin laid on a solid base with nowhere for the water to go is just a different sort of puddle. The surface is one layer of the answer, not the whole of it.

Will tarmac go soft in hot weather?

A new drive can scuff in its first proper hot spell, particularly where a power steering wheel is turned on the spot. It settles with age. Jack stands, bike stands and skip legs are the usual culprits, so put a board under anything with a point on it for the first summer.

This is not an emergency trade, and we will not pretend it is

Nobody is coming out at midnight to lay blocks. What we will do is get a trip hazard made safe quickly, then book the proper repair in daylight.

Make it safe first

  1. Mark it so people can see it. A cone, a bucket, a chair, a bag of sand, anything that stops somebody walking into it in the dark.
  2. If a flag is rocking, lift it right out rather than leaving it to tip under somebody's foot, and stand it against the wall.
  3. Keep the route to the front door clear, even if that means going round the long way for a few days.
  4. Take a photograph with something in it for scale, a shoe or a tape, before anything is moved.

If it is on the pavement rather than your drive

A lifted or broken footway flag outside your boundary belongs to the council, and it is their job to repair it. Report it to them. Do not pay anybody to mend the public footway.

Making safe is not the repair. A sunken patch is lifted, the layer underneath is put right and the same blocks or flags go back down. That gets booked properly rather than rushed in the dark.
