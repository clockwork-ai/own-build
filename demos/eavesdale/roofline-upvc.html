Roofline replacement in uPVC

Roofline replacement in uPVC.

Why all of it at once

Houses do not rot one elevation at a time. The timber round a house is the same age, painted the same number of times, and facing weather that varies by aspect rather than by much else. So when the front needs doing, the back is usually two or three years behind it rather than twenty.

The real argument for doing the lot is the access. Scaffold and towers are a serious part of this work, and the cost barely changes whether you do one elevation or all of them while it is up. Do the front this year and the gable in two years and you pay for the access twice, and you get a house where one side matches and the other does not.

What a full roofline includes

The fasciaThe board the gutter hangs offThe upright board along the front edge of the roof, closing off the ends of the rafters. It is not decoration: every gutter bracket on the house screws into it, so when the timber goes soft the brackets start pulling and the run begins to drop. If a gutter has sagged between two brackets, look at the board behind it before you look at the gutter.
The soffitThe board underneath, and the airThe flat board running from the fascia back to the wall. It closes the gap under the roof edge so birds and wasps stay out, and it carries the ventilation. Air has to get into the roof space along that line, which is why a new soffit has a vent strip in it. Board it over solid and you can swap a rotten fascia for condensation in the loft.
The gutterCatching it and moving it alongLengths clipped into brackets, joined with sealed unions, falling gently to a running outlet. The fall is small, a few millimetres a metre, and it is set with a line rather than by eye. Get it wrong and water stands in the middle of the run, which is why some gutters overflow in the same spot every year with nothing blocking them.
The downpipeGetting it to the ground and awayFrom the outlet, usually with a swan neck to clear the eaves, down the wall on clips and into a gully. The two things that matter are the clips, which belong in the mortar joints rather than the brick face, and the bottom, which should go into the drain rather than splash on to a path next to the wall.

What we look at while it is open

Taking the old boards off is the only time in decades that the edge of your roof is open, so it gets a proper look: rafter feet, the felt at the eaves and whether it has perished where it turns into the gutter, the wall plate, birds and wasps, and whether the tiles at the bottom course are sitting as they should.

You get told all of it with photographs. Some of it we put right as part of the job. Some of it is a roofer's work and we will say so plainly rather than taking it on. And some of it is fine and worth knowing is fine, because a straight answer about good timber is worth as much as a warning about bad.

Colour, profile and the dull decisions

White board with white gutter is the common one. Black gutter on white board has become just as common and hides staining better. Anthracite, woodgrain and a cast iron effect all exist, and on an older stone house the cast iron effect usually looks right where bright white does not.

The decision that matters more than colour is the gutter profile, because that is the one that decides whether the run can carry what your roof throws at it. Where a house has a big steep roof, or two roofs running into one gutter, going back with the same small half round is how you end up with a brand new roofline that still goes over in a downpour.

On the job

How this one usually goes.

  1. Measure up and set out the accessEvery elevation, the gable, and how we get to each run safely. The access goes on the quote as its own line so you can see what it is.
  2. Strip one elevation at a timeGutter down, old board off, and the house never left with more open at the end of a day than we can cover if it rains.
  3. Look at the roof edge and say what is thereRafter feet, eaves felt, wall plate, birds. Photographs of all of it, including the parts that are perfectly sound.
  4. Soffit, fascia, bargeboard, claddingIn that order, vents in, fixed into sound timber, and the gable done on the same scaffold rather than left for another year.
  5. Gutters and pipes to a line, then testedBrackets to a string line so the fall is right, outlets where the water actually needs to leave, pipes clipped into mortar, and water run through every run.
  6. Clear the site and wash the wall downOld timber and offcuts away with us, drive swept, and the brickwork washed where it has been stained for years ${S}.

Questions

Asked about this job.

How long does a full roofline take?

Usually two to three days on an ordinary semi with a gable, mostly set by the access rather than the boards. A detached house with more elevations and a longer scaffold is longer. Heavy rain stops it rather than slowing it, because we will not leave the roof edge open in it.

Does it have to be white?

No. White, black gutter on white board, anthracite, woodgrain and a cast iron effect are all ordinary choices. On an older stone house the cast iron effect tends to look right where bright white looks wrong, and we will give you an honest opinion if you ask for one.

Will you find things wrong with my roof?

Sometimes, and you will be told with a photograph. Perished felt at the eaves and soft rafter ends are the two most common. Some of that is part of this job, some of it wants a roofer, and we will be clear about which rather than taking on work that is not ours.

Can I do it in stages?

Yes, and we will price it both ways. The thing to weigh is that the access is the part that does not get cheaper by being split, so two visits normally costs more in total than one. If money is the reason for staging it, say so and we will tell you which elevation genuinely needs doing first.

Nobody should be up a ladder in this

Not us and not you. In wind or heavy rain a ladder against a wet wall is how people get hurt, and a gutter overflowing for one more night does far less damage than a fall. The job tonight is to move the water away from the house and the people, and book the repair for the first dry morning.

What to do now

  1. Find where it is going over and put something under it. A bucket, a bin lid, a length of board leaning against the wall to throw the water out away from the brickwork.
  2. If a downpipe is hanging loose and you can reach it safely from the ground, tie it back against the wall so it cannot swing, and keep everybody out from underneath.
  3. Move anything that is getting soaked: bins, bikes, the dog bed by the back door, anything stacked against that bit of wall.
  4. Take a photograph or a short video from the ground while it is actually raining. Water tells you more in thirty seconds of rain than in an hour of dry weather.

If it is coming in rather than running down outside

Water showing inside, on a ceiling or down a chimney breast, is not a roofline job and may not be the gutter at all. Catch the drips, keep away from anything wet near electrics, and ring. We will tell you honestly if it wants a roofer instead, and we would rather say so than have a go.
