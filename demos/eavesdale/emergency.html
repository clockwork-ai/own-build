Pouring over, or hanging off?

Roofline work is not a midnight trade and we are not going to pretend it is. Nobody should be up a ladder in wind or driving rain, including us. What follows is how to take the sting out of it tonight and get the repair booked for the first dry morning.

First

Make it safe, not mended.

Do this now

  1. Find where the water is coming over and put something under it: a bucket, a bin lid, a board leaning against the wall to throw it clear of the brickwork.
  2. If a downpipe is swinging and you can reach it safely from the ground, tie it back against the wall so it cannot move, and keep people from underneath it.
  3. Move whatever is getting soaked. Bins, bikes, boxes, anything stacked against that strip of wall.
  4. Take a photograph or a short video from the ground while it is raining. Thirty seconds of rain shows more than an hour of dry weather.
  5. If anything is loose above a path or a door, keep everybody off that route until it has been looked at.

Do not do this

  • Do not put a ladder up in wind or heavy rain. This is the one that hurts people, and the gutter will still be there in the morning.
  • Do not go on the roof. Not for a gutter, not for anything.
  • Do not climb on a conservatory roof, a flat roof or a lean to to reach a gutter. They are not made to stand on.
  • Do not poke a blockage down the downpipe. You turn a gutter full of silt into a blocked pipe you cannot reach.
  • Do not pull a hanging pipe off the wall to get it down. The clips are usually still holding brick, and it comes away in pieces.

The three we get rung about

What each one actually means.

Gutter overflowing in heavy rain

Nearly always a blockage sat at the outlet, and almost never something that has to be fixed in the dark. Get a bucket or a board under it to keep the water off the wall, then let it be. If it only ever goes over in the heaviest rain and runs clear the rest of the time, say so when you ring, because that pattern points at volume rather than blockage and it is a different job.

A fallen or hanging downpipe

The pipe is not the worry, the wall is: everything it was carrying is now going into the brickwork. Tie it back if you can reach from the ground, keep people out from underneath, and leave the clips alone. The repair is new clips into the mortar joints, and finding what wet the wall and let the old ones go.

A loose fascia or board

If a length of board or a bargeboard is lifting, the fixings have lost their grip on timber that has gone soft. In wind it can work further loose. Keep the route under it clear, do not try to screw it back from a ladder, and tell us which elevation. A board that has let go is usually the start of the fascia conversation rather than a one hour repair.

What making it safe actually means

It means the water is going somewhere that is not your wall or your doorway, and nobody is going to walk under something that might come down. That is all it means tonight. It is not the repair, and treating it as one is how people end up on a ladder in the dark.

The reason we say this plainly is that the damage from a gutter overflowing for one night is small and slow. Water against brickwork for a night is a wet patch. Water against brickwork for three winters is the thing that takes plaster off inside. The urgency is in getting it looked at within a week or two, not within an hour.

When it is not a roofline job at all

If water is showing inside the house, on a ceiling, round a window head or down a chimney breast, do not assume it is the gutter. It might be, and it might be the roof, the flashing or a window. Catch the drips, keep clear of anything wet near electrics, and ring. We will tell you honestly when it wants a roofer instead of us, and we would far rather do that than guess and charge you for the wrong visit.

Rented houses

If you rent, this is your landlord's or your agent's job and the repair is not yours to arrange. Tell them in writing, in a message or an email rather than a phone call, so there is a record of when you told them and what you said. Photographs with it. If somebody could get hurt, say that in the message too.

Questions

Asked at the worst moment.

It is pouring over the front edge right now. Should I get up there?

No. Put something under it to keep the water off the wall and off the path, and leave it until it is dry and calm. A gutter blocked tonight will still be blocked in the morning, and almost nothing about a night of overflow is worse than a fall from a wet ladder.

When you ring, say whether it goes over all the time or only in the heaviest rain, and whether it is in one spot or along the run. Those two answers tell us whether to expect a blockage, a fall problem or a gutter that is simply too small for the roof above it.

My downpipe is swinging in the wind. What do I do?

If you can reach it standing on the ground, tie it back against the wall with anything that will hold, and keep everybody from walking underneath. If you cannot reach it from the ground, leave it and keep the area clear.

Do not pull it off the wall to get it down and do not go up after it in wind. The clips are usually still gripping brick, so it tends to come away in pieces and take a bit of the brickwork with it.

Water is coming through my ceiling. Is that the gutter?

It might be, and it might not. A blocked gutter on a shallow eaves can push water back under the tiles, so it does happen. So does a slipped tile, failed flashing, a leaking window head and condensation in a cold loft.

Catch the drips, move what is underneath, and stay away from anything wet near a light fitting or a socket. Then ring and say what you can see outside as well as inside. If it wants a roofer rather than us, we will say so.

Will somebody come out tonight?

On this demo, no, because the firm is not real and the number rings nobody. On a real site the honest answer from a roofline firm is usually first thing on the next dry morning, not tonight, and any firm promising a time in a storm is promising something the weather decides.

There are no response times anywhere on this site on purpose. Inventing one would be making up a figure about a business that does not exist.

Is an overflowing gutter actually doing damage?

Over one night, not much. Over a few winters, yes, and it is the slow kind nobody notices until it shows inside. Water running down brickwork keeps the wall wet, it gets into the mortar joints, it freezes, and it does its worst at the point where the wall meets the roof or a window head.

The other damage is to the fascia behind the gutter. Water that goes over the back edge rather than the front sits against the board, and that is how a timber fascia goes soft enough to let the brackets pull out.
