Repair first, and mean it

Most old sash windows are in better shape than they look. Paint and putty go long before timber does, and a window that has been painted shut for fifteen years is usually a sound window with a bad decade behind it. Cut out the rot that is there, let in new timber, re-cord it, ease the beads, draught strip the gaps and it works again.

What makes a sash window miserable to live with is rattle, draught and the fact it will not stay up. All three are fixable. New cords and correctly weighted sashes make a window that slides with one finger and stays where you put it. Brush seals in the beads take the whistle out of a winter night without the window looking any different from the street.

When replacing is the right call

When the box frame itself has gone soft and the rot runs back into the wall, when the sashes are patched more than they are timber, or when somebody has already replaced them badly with something that does not open. Then it is a new sash window, and the thing to get right is the sightlines: the width of the meeting rail, the depth of the bottom rail, the horns, the glazing bars, and how far back the window sits in the reveal.

Timber or uPVC is a real choice here. A timber sash looks right on an older house and wants painting every few years. A modern uPVC sliding sash takes less looking after and, from the pavement, convinces far more people than it used to, as long as the sightlines are right. On a conservation area house or a listed building, what you are allowed to do is decided by the council, so that is the first phone call, not the last.

What we will not pretend

We will not tell you an original window is beyond saving to sell you a new one, and we will not patch a frame that is past patching so the problem comes back in two winters. Both are easy money and both are the reason people mistrust this trade.

On the day

How this one usually goes.

  1. A proper look at the timberWhere the rot actually is, usually the bottom rail and the cill. A screwdriver tells you more than a photograph.
  2. Repair or replace, said plainlyWhich sashes can be saved, which need letting in, and whether the box frame is sound. If it is a replacement, you hear why.
  3. Cords, weights and balanceNew cords, the weights matched to the sash so it stays where you put it, and the pulleys cleaned or renewed.
  4. Easing and draught proofingBeads eased so the sashes run without binding, brush seals set in, and the rattle taken out without the window looking any different.
  5. Painted and left workingMade good, and both sashes slid up and down with you watching. A sash window you are afraid to open is not fixed.

Questions

Asked about this job.

Can my sash windows be saved?

More often than not. Paint, putty and cords fail long before timber does. The honest test is the bottom rail and the cill, and that takes somebody looking at them rather than guessing from a photo.

Will draught proofing stop the rattle?

Yes, that is mostly what it does. Brush seals in the beads and the meeting rail close the gaps the sashes rattle in, and the window still opens as it should. It is one of the cheapest things you can do to an old window.

Can I have uPVC sashes that look like the originals?

Modern sliding sash uPVC is far better than it was, and from the street it convinces most people, so long as the sightlines and the horns are right. Up close, timber still looks like timber. We will show you both and let you decide.

What about a conservation area or a listed building?

Ring the council before anything is ordered. What you may change is decided there, and it varies street by street. We will work to whatever they tell you, but we cannot overrule it and nor can anybody else quoting you.

If anybody is cut, that comes first

Ring 999 for a bad cut. Glass injuries bleed more than people expect, and a window can wait.

If it was a break in, ring the police before you tidy

101 to report it, or 999 if you think somebody is still there. Leave the damage as it is until they have been, and take photographs for your insurer before anything is moved.

While you wait

  1. Keep children and animals out of the room and put shoes on.
  2. Leave glass that is still standing in the frame where it is. Pulling at it is how people get hurt.
  3. Sweep up what has fallen with a dustpan, then go over it with a damp cloth or a vacuum for the splinters.
  4. If a door will not lock, move anything worth taking out of sight and use another door until we have been.

We make it safe first: glass cleared, the opening boarded or temporary glass in, and the door shutting and locking again. The proper repair comes after, in daylight, once we know what is needed.
