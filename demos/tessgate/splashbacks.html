Kitchen splashbacks

Kitchen splashbacks.

The strip of wall between the worktop and the units. Small, awkward, full of sockets and corners, and the only tiling in the house that people stand two feet away from every single day.

Get a quote

Why a small job is not an easy one

A splashback is often less than two square metres, which makes people assume it is a morning's work for very little. The area is small. The number of decisions is not.

Nearly every one has sockets in it, and a socket in the wrong place in a tile pattern is the first thing a visitor's eye goes to. It has an internal corner at each end, an external corner somewhere, and a junction with the worktop that moves every time somebody leans on it. It usually has to finish at a cooker, a window reveal or the underside of a wall unit, and those edges all need a decision. And because it is at eye level and an arm's length away, there is nowhere for a bad cut to hide.

Where the cuts go

On a splashback the set out is dictated by the things in it rather than by the wall. The joints get lined up on the sockets, the switch plates and the extractor where we can, because that is what the eye uses as a reference. Then the course heights get worked out from the worktop up, so the top course finishes at a sensible line under the units rather than as a two centimetre strip.

If there is a pattern, a brick bond or a herringbone, the set out matters more again, because a pattern that is out by half a tile at one end tells on itself across a short run far more than it does across a bathroom wall.

Edges, and what happens at them

The junction with the worktop gets a flexible sealant bead, not grout, every time. A worktop moves, a sink gets loaded and unloaded, and the grout in that joint will split within the year and then start staining.

External corners get a trim, a mitre or a bullnose tile depending on the tile and what you want it to look like, and it is worth deciding that before anything is ordered because not every tile can be mitred. Where the splashback stops in mid wall there has to be a deliberate edge rather than a raw cut, and where it meets a window reveal or a cooker there is usually a trim or a bead. We will walk through those with you rather than making the decision quietly on the day.

The internal corner

The one joint that moves.

Two tiled surfaces meeting in a corner, looked at from above. Everything else on a tiled wall holds still. This one does not, and that changes what goes in it.

It splits

Grout in the internal corner

Grout is hard and it does not stretch. Two walls meeting in a corner move a fraction against each other with the seasons, and a bath or a tray moves every time somebody gets in it. So the grout splits along the corner, usually within a year, and then it is an open crack straight into the wall rather than a joint.

It stays shut

Flexible sealant in the internal corner

Every internal corner, every junction with a bath, a tray or a worktop, and anywhere tile meets a different material. A flexible bead takes the movement and stays closed. It is not a worse finish than grout, it is the right material for a joint that moves, and it is the single most common thing we find done wrong on a bathroom somebody else tiled.

On the job

How this one usually goes.

  1. Look at the wall and the socketsWhere the sockets, switches and the extractor are, where the worktop runs, what the units do at each end and whether the wall behind is flat and sound.
  2. Agree the edgesWhat happens at the external corner, where the run stops, what meets the cooker and the window. Decided before anything is ordered, because the tile has to suit the answer.
  3. PrepareAnything loose taken off, the wall made flat and primed, and the worktop and the units protected properly, because this one happens in a kitchen people still need to use.
  4. Set outJoints lined through the sockets where we can, course heights worked from the worktop up, and a decision made about the top course before a tile goes on.
  5. Tile and cutCuts for the sockets done on the saw rather than nibbled, the run checked level all the way across, and the mess kept to the one bit of worktop we are working on.
  6. Grout and sealJoints grouted, internal corners and the whole worktop junction run in flexible sealant, and the worktop cleaned off before we leave.

Questions

Asked about this job.

Can you tile round the sockets neatly?

Yes, and it is one of the few places on a small job where a saw beats a hand cutter. The cut goes to the plate, the plate covers the edge, and the joints are set out so they run through the sockets rather than past them at an awkward angle.

On a real job the sockets usually come off the wall first and go back after, which is an electrician's bit rather than ours. We will say what wants doing and by whom rather than working round a plate and leaving a visible gap.

How high should a splashback go?

The usual is from the worktop up to the underside of the wall units, which keeps it simple and is what most kitchens want. Behind a hob people often take it higher, sometimes to the extractor or to the ceiling, and that reads well as long as the edge where it stops is a deliberate line rather than wherever the tile ran out.

On a run with no wall units above it, the height is a design decision and we would rather you picked a line off something in the room, the top of a window reveal or the top of a door, than a number.

Can you tile over the existing splashback?

Same answer as anywhere else: tap it first. If it is solid, flat and well stuck it can be tiled onto once it is keyed and primed. The thing to check on a kitchen is the extra thickness at the worktop, at the window reveal and anywhere the tile has to die into a unit, because a splashback is all edges and twenty millimetres shows up fast.

Over a glass or acrylic splashback panel, no. That comes off.

What about herringbone or a patterned tile?

Happily, and it suits a splashback because the run is short enough for a pattern to read without getting busy. It takes longer and there are more cuts, so it goes on the quote as what it is rather than as the same price as a straight bond.

Order extra. A herringbone or any diagonal pattern wastes more tile in cuts than people expect, and running short of a dye lot halfway along a splashback is a bad afternoon.

Will it be done in a day?

A plain splashback onto a sound flat wall, often yes for the tiling, with the grouting and sealing the morning after so the adhesive has gone off properly. We would rather come back for an hour than grout something that is still moving.

If the wall needs taking back, making flat or boarding, or if there is a pattern or a lot of cuts, it is two days. You will be told which on the quote.

Tiling is not an emergency trade, with one exception

Almost nothing a tiler does cannot wait a fortnight. The exception is a cracked or loose tile in a shower or behind a bath where water is already getting past it, because that water is going somewhere and it is usually into the wall, the floor or the ceiling below.

Stop using that shower first

  1. Use another shower or a bath for now. Every use puts more water behind the tile.
  2. Run a temporary bead of sealant over the crack or the open joint if you have a tube in the house. It is not a repair, it buys you days.
  3. Look at the ceiling in the room underneath. A stain or a soft patch there changes this from a tiling job into something bigger.
  4. Ring and say which it is, so the right person comes.

What we will not do

Print a response time. There is no number on this site for how fast anybody turns up, because any number we put here would be made up. Ring and you will be told honestly when somebody can be there.

If water is coming through a ceiling, or the floor is soft underfoot, that is past a tiler. Get the water off first, then the damage looked at, then the tiling.
