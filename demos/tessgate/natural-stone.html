Natural stone tiling

Natural stone tiling.

Slate, limestone, travertine and marble. Heavier, thirstier and more varied than porcelain, so it wants sorting before it goes down, sealing before it is grouted and a floor underneath that can carry it.

Get a quote

Stone is not porcelain that costs more

It is a different material and it wants treating like one. It came out of the ground rather than a factory, so every piece is a bit different in colour, in thickness and sometimes in size. That variation is the entire reason people want it and it is also the reason it takes longer to lay.

Before anything goes down, the whole lot gets opened and dry laid out so the colours and the markings are spread across the floor rather than ending up with all the dark ones in one corner. On a riven stone like slate the thicknesses vary too, so the bed gets adjusted tile by tile rather than combed once and forgotten.

Thirsty, and what that means

Most natural stone is porous. It will take up water, and it will take up wine, oil, coffee and anything else that lands on it. That is why stone gets sealed, and it is also why the sealing happens before the grout goes in as well as after: unsealed stone will suck the pigment out of grout and hold onto it, and a grout haze on a porous stone can be very hard to shift.

Some stones have their own quirks on top of that. Anything with lime in it, which includes limestone, travertine and marble, reacts with acid, so a vinegar based cleaner or a strong bathroom spray will etch the surface and leave a dull mark. Travertine arrives with natural holes in it that are usually filled, and those fills can come out over the years and want topping up. Worth knowing before you choose, not after.

Weight, and the floor underneath

Stone is heavier than porcelain and usually thicker. On a solid concrete floor that is not an issue. On a timber floor it very much can be, and it is the thing we check first.

A timber floor has to be stiff enough to carry the weight without flexing, which often means additional boarding or sometimes work on the joists, and that is a conversation to have before anybody orders a pallet of stone. On top of that, timber moves with the seasons and stone does not, so a decoupling membrane goes between them to let one move without cracking the other. On a heated floor it is the same answer again. In section

What is under a heated tiled floor.

Five layers, bottom to top, each one doing a different job. The one people leave out is the fourth, and it is the one that stops the floor underneath taking the tiles with it when it moves.

1The tile 2Flexible adhesive 3Heating mat 4Decoupling membrane 5The floor you already have
1. The tilePorcelain or stone. On a heated floor the tile wants to be a good conductor and happy with the temperature going up and down, which is nearly all porcelain and most stone, and is one reason nobody puts a timber floor over heating without a lot more thought.
2. Flexible adhesiveNot the standard stuff. A heated floor grows and shrinks a little every time it comes on and goes off, and an adhesive that cannot take that will let go at the edges first. Full bed under every tile, not dabs, because an air pocket over a heating cable is a hot spot.
3. The heatingA thin electric mat or wet pipework in a screed, taped or clipped down so nothing moves while the floor is laid. It goes in before the tile and it is tested before, during and after, because the one thing you cannot do afterwards is get at it.
4. The decoupling membraneThe layer people leave out, and the one that saves the floor. It lets the floor underneath move without dragging the tiles with it, and it gives any damp a route out sideways instead of up through the joints. On a timber floor, over heating, or on anything that has moved before, it is not optional.
5. The floor you already haveBoards, chipboard, concrete or an old screed. This gets checked first: level, solid, no bounce, nothing loose. A floor that flexes a few millimetres under your heel will crack a tile or a grout line no matter what goes on top of it, and that is the honest conversation to have before anybody quotes.

On the job

How this one usually goes.

  1. Check the floor will take itWeight, stiffness, bounce and what is underneath. On timber this is the first question and sometimes the answer changes the stone.
  2. Dry lay the whole lotEvery box opened and the tiles spread out, so the colours and the markings are shared across the room rather than landing in patches.
  3. Prepare and decoupleFloor made flat and solid, boarding or levelling where needed, then a decoupling membrane on timber, over heating, or anywhere that has moved before.
  4. Seal before fixingMost stone is sealed before it goes down, so the grout cannot stain it. On a porous stone this step is the difference between a floor you can live with and one you cannot clean.
  5. Fix, adjusting as we goFull bed under every tile, and on a riven stone the bed adjusted piece by piece because the thicknesses vary. Checked flat constantly rather than at the end.
  6. Grout, clean off and seal againGrout chosen to suit the stone, cleaned off before it grabs, then a second seal over the finished floor once everything has dried out.
  7. Tell you how to look after itWhich cleaners are fine, which ones will etch it, and roughly when it will want sealing again. In writing, because nobody remembers it from a doorstep conversation.

Questions

Asked about this job.

Does natural stone need sealing?

Nearly always, and usually twice: once before it is laid so the grout cannot stain it, and again over the finished floor. How often it wants doing after that depends on the stone and where it is. A busy kitchen floor is a different answer to a spare bathroom.

The quick test at home is to put a few drops of water on the stone and leave it a few minutes. If it darkens where the water sat, the seal has worn off that patch and it is due.

Can stone go in a shower?

Yes, and it looks superb, with two honest warnings. Porous stone in a shower wants sealing properly and resealing more often than anywhere else in the house, because it gets wet several times a day.

And anything with lime in it, so limestone, travertine and marble, will be etched by an acidic cleaner, which includes a lot of ordinary limescale sprays. In a hard water area that is a real consideration. We will tell you which stones we would and would not put in a shower rather than just tiling what you bought.

Can you put stone over underfloor heating?

Yes, and stone is good over heating because it holds the warmth. It needs a flexible adhesive, a decoupling membrane and a full bed under every tile with no voids over the cables.

Stone is also thicker than porcelain, so it takes longer to warm up and longer to cool down. That is not a fault, it just means the heating wants running on a timer rather than being switched on when you walk in. There is a drawing of the build up below.

Why are the tiles not all the same size?

Because it is stone. Calibrated stone is cut to a consistent thickness and is much easier to lay flat. Riven stone like slate is split along its natural layers, so the face is uneven and the thickness varies across one tile, never mind between two.

That is handled in the bed rather than by picking out the odd ones. It is slower, and it is why a riven slate floor costs more to lay than a porcelain one of the same area.

What cleaners can I use on it?

Something with a neutral pH, and nothing acidic on anything with lime in it. That rules out a lot of supermarket limescale and bathroom sprays on limestone, travertine and marble, and it is the commonest way a good stone floor gets ruined.

We will write down what suits the actual stone you have got when the job is done, rather than leaving you to guess in an aisle.

Tiling is not an emergency trade, with one exception

Almost nothing a tiler does cannot wait a fortnight. The exception is a cracked or loose tile in a shower or behind a bath where water is already getting past it, because that water is going somewhere and it is usually into the wall, the floor or the ceiling below.

Stop using that shower first

  1. Use another shower or a bath for now. Every use puts more water behind the tile.
  2. Run a temporary bead of sealant over the crack or the open joint if you have a tube in the house. It is not a repair, it buys you days.
  3. Look at the ceiling in the room underneath. A stain or a soft patch there changes this from a tiling job into something bigger.
  4. Ring and say which it is, so the right person comes.

What we will not do

Print a response time. There is no number on this site for how fast anybody turns up, because any number we put here would be made up. Ring and you will be told honestly when somebody can be there.

If water is coming through a ceiling, or the floor is soft underfoot, that is past a tiler. Get the water off first, then the damage looked at, then the tiling.
