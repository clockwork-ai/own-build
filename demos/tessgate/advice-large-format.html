Large format tiles: what to know first.

Fewer joints, more preparation. Why the quote for a one metre tile is not the same as the quote for a small one, and what you are actually paying for.

Generated for this demo. Not a photograph of a real job.

Why people want them

Fewer grout lines. That is really the whole of it, and it is a good reason. A small bathroom tiled in a big format tile has a handful of joints instead of a few hundred, and the room reads as calmer and usually feels bigger. There is less grout to keep clean, which in a shower is a genuine practical gain rather than just a look. And a big tile carries a pattern, a marble veining or a concrete finish much better than a small one, because you see the pattern rather than a grid with pattern inside it.

Why the quote is higher

Because almost none of the extra cost is the tile. It is these, and they are all real:

Two people. A large tile cannot be lifted and placed accurately by one person, so every hour on site is two hours of labour. A suction frame, a bigger trowel, often back buttering as well as combing, which means noticeably more adhesive. Levelling clips across every joint, which are consumable and which take time to fit and knock off. A wet saw for every cut, set up outside, because nothing that size goes on a hand cutter. And a higher risk on breakages: one tile broken is a bigger loss and sometimes a week's wait for another.

And underneath all of those, the one that actually costs the most: making the wall or the floor flat enough.

Flat, and what it actually means

Every wall in every house has high spots and hollows in it. Run a long straightedge across one and you will see daylight under it somewhere. That is normal and it has never mattered much, because most things that go on a wall are flexible or small.

A small tile is effectively small enough not to care: it spans a few inches, so it drops into the hollow and comes back out again and the grout line absorbs the difference. A tile a metre and a bit long cannot do that. It is rigid and it is long, so it bridges from one high spot to the next. That leaves a void behind the middle of it, which is a weak spot that will crack if something lands on it, and it lifts one end proud of the tile next to it.

That step between two tiles is a lip. You feel it with your hand long before you see it, and on a floor you feel it through your feet and catch a mop on it. There is no technique that avoids it on an out of true wall. The only answer is to make the background flat first, and the drawing below shows why.

What to ask any firm quoting it

Ask whether they have put a straightedge on the walls and the floor. If nobody has, the quote is a guess and the levelling will appear later as an extra.

Ask what joint width they are recommending and why. A big tile is never perfectly flat or perfectly square, and the joint is where that tolerance lives. A two to three millimetre joint on a wall is sensible. A hairline joint looks sharp in a photograph and leaves nothing to adjust, which makes any lipping much more obvious.

Ask whether they are back buttering as well as combing, and whether the external corners are being mitred or trimmed, because not every tile will take a mitre. And ask what happens if one breaks: how long another one takes to arrive, and who carries that.

When a smaller tile is the honest answer

If the walls are badly out and the budget will not stretch to levelling them, a smaller tile is a better job than a big one fitted hopefully. If the room is tiny and full of pipe boxings, niches and awkward returns, a big tile means a lot of expensive cuts and you lose the main benefit anyway, because you end up with joints wherever the cuts are. And if the floor has any flex in it, that gets fixed before anything, big or small, goes anywhere near it.

A firm that tells you a smaller tile would suit your wall better is not trying to do less work. It is the one answer you cannot get from a brochure.

Tiling is not an emergency trade, with one exception

Almost nothing a tiler does cannot wait a fortnight. The exception is a cracked or loose tile in a shower or behind a bath where water is already getting past it, because that water is going somewhere and it is usually into the wall, the floor or the ceiling below.

Stop using that shower first

  1. Use another shower or a bath for now. Every use puts more water behind the tile.
  2. Run a temporary bead of sealant over the crack or the open joint if you have a tube in the house. It is not a repair, it buys you days.
  3. Look at the ceiling in the room underneath. A stain or a soft patch there changes this from a tiling job into something bigger.
  4. Ring and say which it is, so the right person comes.

What we will not do

Print a response time. There is no number on this site for how fast anybody turns up, because any number we put here would be made up. Ring and you will be told honestly when somebody can be there.

If water is coming through a ceiling, or the floor is soft underfoot, that is past a tiler. Get the water off first, then the damage looked at, then the tiling.
