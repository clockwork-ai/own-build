Dormer or rooflight: which conversion.

The same loft drawn both ways, so you can see what the box on the back actually buys you, and when leaving the roof alone is the better answer.

The short version

If your roof has height and pitch to spare, a rooflight conversion gives you a proper room for less money, less time and less disruption, and nothing changes outside. If it does not, or if you want a flat ceiling and a wall you can stand at, you want a dormer. Most lofts are one or the other fairly obviously once somebody has measured. The ones in between are a genuine decision and that is what this is about.

What a dormer gives you that a rooflight cannot

Not floor area, which is the thing people expect. The floor is the same either way: it is the roof over the floor that changes. What a dormer gives you is a flat ceiling across part of the room and a vertical wall at the end of it.

That matters more than it sounds. You cannot stand at a slope. A wardrobe will not go against one, a basin under one is awkward, and a bookcase is out. A dormer brings a piece of wall into a room that otherwise has none, and the whole layout gets easier. The drawings below show the same loft both ways, and the shaded part is where the ceiling is above standing height.

What a rooflight conversion gives you

Speed, and the outside of your house unchanged. There is no scaffold up for weeks, no roof open to the weather, no planning question on a rear slope in most places, and the saving is real.

It also gives you more daylight per pound than anything else you can do up there, because the sky is brighter than anything at eye level and a window in a slope faces it directly. Two rooflights in one room is normal, three is not extravagant, and they are the cheapest part of the whole job.

The hipped roof case

If your house has a roof that slopes in at the side, the decision is often neither of the above first. A hipped end means the ridge stops short of the end wall and the loft tapers to a point, so the useful floor is a good deal smaller than the footprint suggests. Building that end up as a gable comes first, and then the dormer or rooflight question is about the roof you have left. There is a plan drawing of that below.

How to decide

Measure the height out from the ridge rather than at it. If you have a wide band of standing room, a rooflight conversion will give you a room you are happy with. If the band is a strip, a dormer is what makes it a bedroom. Then weigh up the three things that are not about the room at all: what you want the back of the house to look like, how long you can live with scaffolding, and what the planning position is at your address.

What we would say is that almost nobody regrets a dormer on the back for the room it gave them, and a few regret how big they made it.

Rooflight conversion

Roof line untouched · no scaffold alley · quickest

Nothing changes outside

The roof stays exactly where it is and the windows sit in the plane of the slope. From the street nothing has happened. It is the quickest way up there, the least disruptive, and on a roof with height to spare it gives you a proper room. On a shallow roof like the one drawn here it gives you the strip up the middle and not much either side of it.

Dormer conversion

Flat ceiling · a wall to stand at · the most room

The box is what buys you the room

Build out of the back slope and the ceiling goes flat where the box is, so the floor you can stand on runs from the middle right out to the dormer wall. That wall is the part people underrate: it is somewhere to put a wardrobe, a bed head or a basin without ducking, and you only get it by changing the roof. It needs scaffolding, it takes longer, and from the back of the house it is obvious.

A hipped end

Plan · ridge stops short · floor tapers away

The end of the loft is the bit you cannot use

On a hipped roof the ridge stops short of the end wall and the roof slopes in from three directions instead of two. In plan that means the useful floor comes to a point well before the end of the house, and the last stretch is a wedge too low to do anything with. It is why so many thirties semis look as though they have plenty of loft and turn out to have one usable room's worth.

Built up to a gable

Plan · ridge runs through · floor goes square

The end wall comes up and the point disappears

The hip comes off, the end wall is built up square in brick or stone to match, and the ridge is carried through to it. The shaded part is the floor that was a wedge before. It is usually the difference between one room up there and a room with a shower room next to it, and on a semi it is the single change that alters the plan most.

Questions

Asked about this.

Can I start with rooflights and add a dormer later?

Technically yes and in practice it is an expensive way round. The structural work, the scaffold and the inside finishing all get done twice, and the second visit means opening up a room you have just decorated.

If the money only stretches to one now, the better split is usually to do the structure and the floor properly for the room you eventually want and to finish it simply, rather than to do a smaller job that gets undone.

Which one adds more value?

We are not going to put figures on that, partly because this is a demo and partly because anybody who does is guessing. What we would say is what estate agents say: a loft room that is signed off, reached by a proper staircase and big enough to take a double bed and a wardrobe counts as a bedroom. One that is a strip of standing room with sloping walls gets described more carefully.

So the thing that moves the needle is whether it is genuinely a room, which is the same question as which conversion suits your roof.

Is a dormer noisy from inside afterwards?

A flat roof over your head sounds different in heavy rain to a slated slope, and some people like it. Where it goes wrong is a thin flat roof build up with no insulation value doing anything for sound.

Done properly, with a decent depth of insulation and a plasterboard ceiling under it, a dormer is no noisier than the rest of the room. If you are sensitive to it, say so and the build up can be specified accordingly rather than being left as whatever is cheapest.

Take a torch up and have a look

  1. How high is it? Stand a tape on the top of a ceiling joist in the middle and measure straight up to the timber at the ridge. Write the number down rather than judging it.
  2. What is holding the roof up? Slender timbers forming a W across the middle, or two chunky beams running along the slopes with a clear space between them.
  3. Where could the stairs come up? Stand on the landing and look for a run of ceiling long enough for a flight, and which way it would have to climb.
  4. What is up there already? A water tank, a boiler, a soil pipe coming through, old wiring. All moveable, all worth knowing about first.
  5. What is next door? A semi, an end terrace or a mid terrace, and whether anybody on the street has already done theirs.
  6. What is underneath? Which room the stairs would land over, and whether you could stand to lose a slice of it.
