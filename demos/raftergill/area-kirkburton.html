What the lofts are like

Stone houses and bungalows with big roof spaces, where the conversion is often a whole floor rather than one room, so the stairs and the fire route get decided first.

What we would do first

The same thing we do everywhere: somebody up there with a tape, in the middle and at both ends, then down to the landing to work out where a flight could climb and which way. Half an hour, and it is what decides whether a quote is worth anything.

On a street where several houses are the same, it is worth saying so when you ring. Having seen the roof on the house four doors down does not settle your own, because a previous extension or a different internal layout changes it, but it does mean we arrive knowing roughly what to expect.

What we would tell you straight

If the loft will not take a conversion, you will hear it on the first visit, with the reason, before anybody has paid for drawings. Quite often the better job is boarding it out properly with a bigger hatch, a fixed ladder and a light, and that costs a fraction. We would rather do that job well than sell you a conversion that will not work.

Is my loft suitable for a conversion?

Take a torch up and have a look

  1. How high is it? Stand a tape on the top of a ceiling joist in the middle and measure straight up to the timber at the ridge. Write the number down rather than judging it.
  2. What is holding the roof up? Slender timbers forming a W across the middle, or two chunky beams running along the slopes with a clear space between them.
  3. Where could the stairs come up? Stand on the landing and look for a run of ceiling long enough for a flight, and which way it would have to climb.
  4. What is up there already? A water tank, a boiler, a soil pipe coming through, old wiring. All moveable, all worth knowing about first.
  5. What is next door? A semi, an end terrace or a mid terrace, and whether anybody on the street has already done theirs.
  6. What is underneath? Which room the stairs would land over, and whether you could stand to lose a slice of it.

There is no score at the end of this

Six answers written on the back of an envelope, and then somebody comes and measures properly. We will not tell you a loft is suitable off a photograph and we will not print a height on this site as though it were a rule, because the rule depends on your roof, your stairs and what the drawings come back saying. We measure, and then we tell you straight.

If the answer is no, you will hear it on the first visit rather than after a set of drawings. A boarded loft with a proper hatch and a light is often the better job anyway, and it costs a fraction.
