About

A firm that does not exist, built properly.

Raftergill Lofts is invented. There is no yard, no van, no team and no customers. What is real is the site: the pages, the drawings, the chat box and the way it is all put together, because that is the part Clockwork AI builds and this is the quickest way to show a loft conversion firm what it would get.

Why a loft firm

Loft conversions are a long, expensive, disruptive job that people research for months before they ring anybody. That makes the website do more work than it does for most trades: it has to answer the questions that stop people picking up the phone, and it has to be honest enough that the answers are believed.

So this demo is built around the three things people actually want to know. Whether their loft will work. What the different sorts of conversion do. And what it is like to live in the house while it happens. Everything else on it hangs off those.

The drawings

Six things on this site are drawn in the browser rather than photographed, and they are the reason it reads like a firm that knows the trade. A loft under two different pitches. The same loft with a rooflight and with a dormer. A hipped end in plan against a gable. A staircase climbing the wrong way and then the right way. A trussed roof against a cut roof. And the new floor in section, layer by layer.

They are set out from numbers rather than sketched, so the two halves of each pair share a span and a floor line and holding one against the other is a fair comparison. What none of them carries is a measurement, because the one thing this site will not do is print a head height as though it settled anything.

What is honest about it

Every picture was generated for this demo and every one is labelled as generated. The reviews are made up and labelled as samples. The firm's phone number is from the range Ofcom keeps for television and film, so it rings nobody, and the email ends in .example. The towns are real places and every area page says so. The chat box answers from a script and nothing you type goes anywhere.

There are no prices, no guarantees, no insurance claims, no accreditations, no scheme badges and no years in business, because all of those would have to be invented and inventing them is exactly what a demo should not do.

What a real version would carry instead

Your own photographs, your own jobs with the addresses taken off, your own reviews with the names on, and the things you genuinely hold: the trade memberships, the insurance, the warranty you offer, how long you have been going. That is where a real site gets its weight from, and none of it can be faked into a demo.

Six things to go and look at

Is my loft suitable for a conversion?

Take a torch up and have a look

  1. How high is it? Stand a tape on the top of a ceiling joist in the middle and measure straight up to the timber at the ridge. Write the number down rather than judging it.
  2. What is holding the roof up? Slender timbers forming a W across the middle, or two chunky beams running along the slopes with a clear space between them.
  3. Where could the stairs come up? Stand on the landing and look for a run of ceiling long enough for a flight, and which way it would have to climb.
  4. What is up there already? A water tank, a boiler, a soil pipe coming through, old wiring. All moveable, all worth knowing about first.
  5. What is next door? A semi, an end terrace or a mid terrace, and whether anybody on the street has already done theirs.
  6. What is underneath? Which room the stairs would land over, and whether you could stand to lose a slice of it.

There is no score at the end of this

Six answers written on the back of an envelope, and then somebody comes and measures properly. We will not tell you a loft is suitable off a photograph and we will not print a height on this site as though it were a rule, because the rule depends on your roof, your stairs and what the drawings come back saying. We measure, and then we tell you straight.

If the answer is no, you will hear it on the first visit rather than after a set of drawings. A boarded loft with a proper hatch and a light is often the better job anyway, and it costs a fraction.
