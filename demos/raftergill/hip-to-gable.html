Hip to gable conversions

Hip to gable conversions.

For the semi, the end terrace or the bungalow where the roof slopes in at the side and the loft runs out to a point. The end wall gets built up square, the ridge carries through, and the wedge becomes room.

Why a hipped roof wastes a loft

A hipped roof slopes in on three or four sides instead of two. It looks tidy from the street and it is a sensible roof, and inside it means the ridge stops short of the end wall and the space tapers away to nothing before it gets there. On a typical thirties semi that is a surprising amount of the loft gone.

Build the end up as a gable, which is to say a plain square wall up to the ridge, and the roof becomes a simple two sided one. The ridge runs the whole length, the taper disappears, and the floor goes square to the end wall. There is a drawing of it in plan below, because plan is the view that shows what you actually get.

It is structural work, not an extension

A hip is doing a job. Taking it off and building a wall up in its place changes the way the roof is held, and that is engineering rather than carpentry. The gable is built in matching brick or stone off the existing wall below, which has to be able to carry it, so the engineer looks at what is underneath as well as what is going on top. Then the ridge is extended, new rafters go on, and the roof is tied back into what is there.

This is the conversion type where the drawings and the calculations matter most, and where a quote that has not had an engineer near it should worry you.

Matching the outside

A new gable is a large flat area of wall where there was roof, and getting the materials right is most of whether it looks original in five years. On a brick house that means matching the brick and the bond and using a lime or a cement mortar that matches the colour and the joint. On a gritstone house it means coursed stone that matches, which is slower and dearer and worth it.

The other half is the verge where the new roof meets the new wall, and the rainwater, because a gable end moves where the water goes. New guttering, a downpipe in the right place, and no stains down the new wall in the first winter.

And on a semi, your gable is your neighbour's view. A party wall notice is needed, and telling them in person before a letter arrives is worth more than the notice is.

The end of the loft is the bit you cannot use

On a hipped roof the ridge stops short of the end wall and the roof slopes in from three directions instead of two. In plan that means the useful floor comes to a point well before the end of the house, and the last stretch is a wedge too low to do anything with. It is why so many thirties semis look as though they have plenty of loft and turn out to have one usable room's worth.

On the job

How this one usually goes.

  1. Survey and the engineerUp there with a tape, then a structural engineer on the gable, on what is under it and on the new floor. This type gets more engineering than any other.
  2. Planning and the neighboursThe planning position checked for your address, party wall notices served where there is a shared wall, and the building control application in.
  3. Scaffold and the new floorA full scaffold up the end of the house. Steels in and bedded on padstones, new floor joists, deck down.
  4. The hip comes offSlate off and stacked, the hip timbers taken down, the roof propped and sheeted. The house is temporarily open at that end and that part is planned round the weather.
  5. Gable built upMatching brick or stone up to the ridge line, the ridge extended, new rafters on, and the roof tied back into the existing structure.
  6. Watertight and the rainwaterSlate on, verge detail, lead work, guttering and the downpipe moved to where the water now wants to go.
  7. Inside, stairs, finishInsulation, stairs, plasterboard, skim, second fix, alarms, decorating and the sign off paperwork.

Questions

Asked about this job.

Can you do a hip to gable and a dormer together?

That is the usual pairing and it is where most of the big loft rooms come from: the gable gives you the length and a rear dormer gives you the flat ceiling across the middle. The two jobs share the scaffold and most of the structural work, so doing both at once is a good deal more sensible than doing one and then the other.

Planning is the thing to check. Each has its own rules on size and position, and doing both can take a job past what permitted development allows even when either one on its own would have been fine.

Will the new gable match?

It can be made to match very closely and it will never be invisible on day one, because new brick or stone next to eighty year old brick or stone is new. Give it a couple of winters and it settles in.

What makes the difference is matching the material, the bond and the mortar rather than just the colour, and resisting the urge to clean the old wall to match the new one. On stone, a reclaimed match is usually worth the wait.

Does my neighbour have to agree?

On a semi or a terrace, the gable is on or against a shared wall, so the Party Wall Act applies and notices get served. That is a process with timescales rather than a veto: your neighbour can agree, or appoint a surveyor, and the work goes ahead either way.

The practical advice is to knock on first. Scaffolding up the side of a house for weeks is a lot easier for everybody if the conversation happened before the letter.

Can it be done on a bungalow?

Bungalows are often the best candidates there are, because a hipped bungalow roof usually has a big span and a decent pitch, and the whole first floor is there to be had. Hip to gable plus dormers on a bungalow can double the house.

Two things to check early: whether the walls and the foundations will carry a floor's worth of extra load, which the engineer looks at properly, and where a staircase can go, because a bungalow was not built with a stairwell in it and that is the harder half of the problem.

What about the chimney?

Where a chimney sits in the hip end it has to be dealt with rather than worked round, and the options are leaving it and building the gable to it, taking it down to below the new roof, or removing it altogether. Each has a different cost and a different look.

If it is a shared stack on a semi, or if it still serves a flue in your house or next door, that narrows the options. It gets settled on the drawings, with the engineer, before anything is ordered.

Take a torch up and have a look

  1. How high is it? Stand a tape on the top of a ceiling joist in the middle and measure straight up to the timber at the ridge. Write the number down rather than judging it.
  2. What is holding the roof up? Slender timbers forming a W across the middle, or two chunky beams running along the slopes with a clear space between them.
  3. Where could the stairs come up? Stand on the landing and look for a run of ceiling long enough for a flight, and which way it would have to climb.
  4. What is up there already? A water tank, a boiler, a soil pipe coming through, old wiring. All moveable, all worth knowing about first.
  5. What is next door? A semi, an end terrace or a mid terrace, and whether anybody on the street has already done theirs.
  6. What is underneath? Which room the stairs would land over, and whether you could stand to lose a slice of it.
