Locked out: what to do first.

Five minutes of checking before you ring anybody, and the one thing that stops a lockout turning into a new door.

Generated for this demo. Not a photograph of a real job.

Stop for a minute

Being shut out of your own house makes people do daft things fast, and the daft things are what cost money. The door is not going anywhere. Take five minutes and work through the list below before you ring anybody, including us.

1. Try every other way in

Back door, side door, patio doors, the door from the garage, the gate to the back. If you live with somebody, there is a fair chance one of them went out the back this morning and did not lock it behind them. This is the single most common way a lockout ends, and it is free.

What we would not do is climb. A flat roof, a wheelie bin and a bathroom window at eleven at night is how people end up in A and E instead of in the kitchen.

2. Ring everybody who might have a key

Partner, parent, the neighbour you gave a spare to in 2019 and forgot about, your landlord, the agent, the person who feeds the cat. A spare key ten minutes away beats the best locksmith in the country, and it costs you a text.

3. Work out whether anything is urgent

There is being locked out, and there is being locked out with something happening. A small child or an animal shut in the house, a hob or an oven left on, somebody inside who needs help: those are 999 calls, not locksmith calls. The fire service will get a child out of a house and they will not send you a bill for it.

If none of that applies, you are cold and annoyed rather than in trouble, which is a better place to be making decisions from.

4. Do not force it

This is the bit that turns a lockout into a job. A shoulder to a uPVC door bends the frame and breaks the keeps. A boot near the lock splits the timber round the keep on an older door. A smashed window is dearer than the visit and leaves you with an opening to board up tonight as well.

If a well meaning neighbour appears with a screwdriver and a plan, thank them and say you have somebody coming.

5. Ring, and say the useful things

When you do ring, the things that help are: what sort of door it is, whether you are stood outside it right now, whether anything or anybody is shut in, and your postcode. White uPVC with a handle you lift, a timber door with a key that turns, a composite door, a flat with a communal entrance: each of those is a different set of tools.

Be ready for one more question, which every locksmith worth ringing will ask: can you show in some reasonable way that it is your house. Post with your name, a bill on your phone, a licence, a neighbour who knows you, or your landlord on the phone. Anybody who does not ask you that is somebody to wonder about.

What normally happens next

On most house doors, the door gets opened without damage and the lock still works afterwards. Where that is not possible, the cylinder is what gets sacrificed rather than the door or the frame, and it is replaced there and then. Either way you should be told what is about to happen and why before any of it starts.

One thing to do next week

Get a spare cut and leave it with somebody you trust who lives nearby. It is the cheapest insurance in this trade, and the number of people who do it only after their first lockout is remarkable.

If it is a break in, ring the police first

Ring 101 to report it, or 999 if you think somebody is still in the house or nearby. Get out and stay out until they say otherwise. Then ring us and we will make the house secure. The locks can wait twenty minutes.

If you are locked out

  1. Try every other door and the back gate before anything else. Plenty of lockouts end with a back door somebody forgot was unlocked.
  2. Ring whoever else holds a key. A spare half a mile away beats any locksmith in the country.
  3. If a child, an animal or a hob left on is shut in, say that the moment you ring. If a child is at risk, ring 999 first.
  4. Do not put your shoulder to it, and do not let a neighbour do it for you. A forced door costs more than any lock.
  5. Get somewhere warm and dry if you can, and keep your phone with you.

If your keys were stolen, or lost along with anything that shows your address, say so. That turns the job from getting you in to changing the locks, because somebody is holding a working key.
