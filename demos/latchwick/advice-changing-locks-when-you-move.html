Changing locks when you move house.

Nobody knows how many keys to a house are out there. Here is how to think about it on the week you move in.

Generated for this demo. Not a photograph of a real job.

The question nobody can answer

How many working keys to your new house are out there? The honest answer, on any house that has been lived in, is that nobody knows. The sellers had some. Their grown up children had some. There was a cleaner, a dog walker, a builder, a neighbour who watered the plants. Then go back an owner and start again.

None of those people are coming for you. That is not the point. The point is that you cannot make a list and you cannot get it back, so the only thing you can actually do is make the old keys stop working.

What that involves on a modern door

Less than people expect. On a uPVC or composite door the cylinder is a separate part, held in by one screw, and the long strip of hooks in the edge of the door stays exactly where it is. Swapping it is a short job per door.

On a timber door with a mortice lock it is more involved, because the lock body sits inside the edge of the door, so it either matches the size that is there or the mortice has to be let in to fit the new one. Still a repair rather than a replacement.

While you are at it, get it onto one key

Most houses have collected a key per door over the years and none of them match. If the locks on your outside doors are the sort that take a common key, they can be set up so one key runs the lot. It is the kind of small thing you notice every single day, especially if you are handing a key to a teenager, a cleaner or a carer.

When to do it

Completion day if you can, before the van arrives. An hour on the morning you get the keys is far easier than trying to arrange it around unpacking, and it means the first night you sleep there, you know where every working key is.

If that is not possible, do the doors in this order: the front door, then whichever outside door is least overlooked, then the rest. The back of a house is usually the weaker half and the half nobody can see from the street.

Read your insurance wording

Home insurance policies sometimes say something about the kind of lock they want on an outside door. We are not going to tell you what yours says, because we have not read it. What we would say is: read it, or ring them and ask, before anything gets fitted rather than after. Then tell your locksmith what it says and have them fit to it.

The things people forget

  • The back gate and the side gate, which usually have something on them that has rusted solid.
  • The garage, including the up and over handle, which is often the oldest lock on the property.
  • The shed, which is where the tools to get into the house tend to live.
  • Ground floor windows, which are the cheapest thing on this list to put right.
  • A key safe on the wall left over from a carer or an agent, which somebody still knows the number to.

And then do one more thing

Get a spare cut and leave it with somebody nearby you trust. Nobody does this until the first time they get locked out, and it is the cheapest thing in this entire article.

If it is a break in, ring the police first

Ring 101 to report it, or 999 if you think somebody is still in the house or nearby. Get out and stay out until they say otherwise. Then ring us and we will make the house secure. The locks can wait twenty minutes.

If you are locked out

  1. Try every other door and the back gate before anything else. Plenty of lockouts end with a back door somebody forgot was unlocked.
  2. Ring whoever else holds a key. A spare half a mile away beats any locksmith in the country.
  3. If a child, an animal or a hob left on is shut in, say that the moment you ring. If a child is at risk, ring 999 first.
  4. Do not put your shoulder to it, and do not let a neighbour do it for you. A forced door costs more than any lock.
  5. Get somewhere warm and dry if you can, and keep your phone with you.

If your keys were stolen, or lost along with anything that shows your address, say so. That turns the job from getting you in to changing the locks, because somebody is holding a working key.
