Lock changes and upgrades

Lock changes and upgrades.

New cylinders, new mortice locks, new keys, and doors brought back to one key where that is possible. Usually after moving in, losing a set, or a year of a lock getting stiffer.

The three reasons people ring

Moving in, losing keys, or a lock that has got worse and worse until somebody finally rang about it. The first two are about who holds a key. The third is about the lock itself, and it is the one people leave longest, usually until the key snaps or the door will not open at all.

One key for the whole house

Most houses have collected two or three different keys over the years, one per door, none of them for the same lock. Where the locks will take it, we can set a house up so one key does every outside door. It is a small thing and it makes a daily difference, particularly for anybody who hands keys to a cleaner, a carer or a teenager.

Changing the barrel is not replacing the lock

On a uPVC or composite door, the cylinder is a separate part that slides out once a single screw is undone. The long strip of hooks and rollers in the edge of the door stays where it is. That is why a lock change on a modern door is a short job, and why anybody quoting for a whole new mechanism when all you asked for was new keys is worth questioning.

Timber doors with mortice locks are different. The lock body sits inside the edge of the door, so changing it properly means working to the same size or letting the mortice in to fit. Still a repair, just a longer one.

What we will not claim

We are not going to tell you a particular lock makes a house safe, because no lock does that on its own. What a better lock does is make a quiet forced entry slower and noisier, and most of what we fit is about the weakest point on a house rather than the strongest. Quite often the right answer is to leave the front door alone and spend the money on the back.

On the day

How this one usually goes.

  1. A look round the outsideEvery door that goes outside, and what is on it now. It is common to find three different keys and one door nobody has locked for years.
  2. What you want out of itOne key for everything, or keys for different people, or simply the old set stopped working. It changes what goes on.
  3. Measure and match the partsCylinder length matters more than people think. Sticking out too far is how a lock gets snapped, so it gets measured rather than guessed.
  4. Fit, adjust and testFitted, then the door locked and unlocked with every new key, and the handle checked for how hard it has to work.
  5. Keys handed over and countedYou get told exactly how many keys exist, which is the whole point of changing the lock in the first place.

Questions

Asked about this job.

Should I change my locks after moving house?

We would. You have no idea how many keys to that house exist: the previous owners, their family, a cleaner, a builder, somebody from two owners back.

It is a small job on most doors and it draws a line under all of it. If your home insurance wording says something about the kind of lock it wants on an outside door, read it to us and we will fit to what it says.

Can you get all my doors on one key?

Often, yes. Where the locks on the doors are the kind that take a common key, we can set them up so one key runs the lot.

There are limits. Mixed door types, some patio and french door mechanisms and some older locks will not play. We will tell you what is possible before anything is ordered rather than after.

Do I need to replace the whole mechanism, or just the barrel?

Usually just the barrel. On a uPVC or composite door the cylinder is a part, held by one screw, and the long strip in the edge of the door stays put.

The mechanism only needs doing when the mechanism itself has failed, which is a different symptom: the handle going slack, or the key turning with nothing moving. If somebody is quoting for a whole mechanism when all you asked for was new keys, ask them why.

Is a more expensive lock worth it?

Sometimes, and not for the reason people think. No lock makes a house safe on its own. What a better one does is make a quiet forced entry slower and noisier.

More to the point, it is worth spending on the weakest door rather than the strongest. A good front door and a flimsy back door is the most common thing we see, and the burglar is not going to use the front.

If it is a break in, ring the police first

Ring 101 to report it, or 999 if you think somebody is still in the house or nearby. Get out and stay out until they say otherwise. Then ring us and we will make the house secure. The locks can wait twenty minutes.

If you are locked out

  1. Try every other door and the back gate before anything else. Plenty of lockouts end with a back door somebody forgot was unlocked.
  2. Ring whoever else holds a key. A spare half a mile away beats any locksmith in the country.
  3. If a child, an animal or a hob left on is shut in, say that the moment you ring. If a child is at risk, ring 999 first.
  4. Do not put your shoulder to it, and do not let a neighbour do it for you. A forced door costs more than any lock.
  5. Get somewhere warm and dry if you can, and keep your phone with you.

If your keys were stolen, or lost along with anything that shows your address, say so. That turns the job from getting you in to changing the locks, because somebody is holding a working key.
